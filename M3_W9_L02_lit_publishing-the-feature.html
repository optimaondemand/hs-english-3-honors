📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A finished feature in a Word document has done half its job. The other half is what happens when someone actually opens the page.
Once a feature is final, it can become a real webpage. Today covers what changes between a printed page and a screen: how layout serves reading, where a public-domain image can legally come from and how to caption it, and what a webpage genuinely adds that a printed feature cannot. This lesson covers the move from print to screen.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name what layout choices make a page easier or harder to actually read.
2
I will be able to find a genuinely public-domain image and caption it with its source and rights status.
3
I will be able to explain the difference between writing a feature and presenting one digitally, and where Claude Code may and may not help.
📱 PART 1 · LAYOUT FOR READING, NOT FOR DECORATION
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a webpage you left almost immediately, even though you wanted the information on it. What drove you off?

On a screen, a reader's eye needs the same signposts your printed anatomy already gives them: a clear headline and standfirst at the top, headings that stand out from body text, short paragraphs, a pull-quote large enough to draw a skimming eye, and enough white space that no section feels like a wall. Nothing about "publishing" means adding decoration; it means making the anatomy you already built legible on a different surface.

🖼️ PART 2 · A PUBLIC-DOMAIN IMAGE, FOUND AND CAPTIONED HONESTLY

A webpage version needs at least one visual: a public-domain image with a caption and source, or a simple graphic you build yourself. Wikimedia Commons is the most reliable place to find genuinely public-domain historical images; on any file's page, look for the words "Public domain" in the licensing section, not merely "free to use," which can carry other conditions. Wikimedia Commons hosts the same 1847 title-page image and Brontë portrait already used elsewhere in this module.

A caption for any image you use follows the same shape from the text-features lesson: subject, then origin, then date, then rights status. Example: Jane Eyre, 1847 title page. Smith, Elder and Co. Public domain, Wikimedia Commons.

🌐 PART 3 · WHAT A WEBPAGE ADDS

A printed feature and a webpage carry the same words, but a webpage adds three things a page on paper cannot: it can be reached instantly by a link instead of handed to someone; it can be read at any size, on any device, without a photocopier; and its layout can use color, spacing, and hierarchy to guide the eye in ways a plain document cannot. None of this changes what your argument says. It changes how many strangers actually see it.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING
📓
READING RESPONSE
Find and caption one public-domain image you might use on the webpage (from Wikimedia Commons or another source you can verify), following the subject/origin/date/rights caption shape. Then sketch, in words, a simple three-part layout for your webpage: what goes at the top, what goes in the middle, what goes at the bottom.
🤖 WORKING WITH AI

For a webpage, Claude Code may write the HTML and CSS: building a page is a coding task, and that is different from the rest of the module. What must never change is the feature text itself. Paste the final feature in exactly as written; pull the headline and pull-quote from the writer's own sentences, never ones Claude suggests. If a reader compared the webpage to the feature, the words should read as identical, only the presentation is new.
