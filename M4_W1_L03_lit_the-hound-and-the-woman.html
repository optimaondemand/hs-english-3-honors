📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A machine that sleeps in the firehouse growls at one fireman. A woman in an attic full of books strikes a match herself. Neither is supposed to happen.
A dystopia runs on two engines: comfort, which you studied in the last two lessons, and fear, which you study today. The Mechanical Hound is fear made into a machine, and Bradbury lets you feel it before anyone explains it. Then the firemen answer a call to a house on Elm Street whose owner will not leave, and for the first time in the novel a book is not burned silently. She quotes one. Today's reading tool is a way of reading a scene of violence as a turning point: not what happens, which the plot gives you, but what changes in the person watching, which the prose gives you only if you slow down.

📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time your hands did something before you had decided to do it: caught a falling glass, reached for someone, pulled back. What did that tell you afterward?

Reading a scene of violence as a turning point

When a novel stages something terrible, the plot tells you what happened and the prose tells you what it meant. A turning point is a scene after which a character cannot go back to who he was, and the sign of one is almost always a gap: between what the character does and what he understands, between what he is supposed to feel and what the sentences show him feeling. Read the Elm Street burning with four questions. Who is in control at each step: the men, the woman, the fire, or something in Montag he does not command? What does the prose slow down for, and what does it hurry past? Who speaks, and what do they say that no one is supposed to say? And afterward, what does the character carry away, in his hands or in his mind, that he did not carry in?

THE MECHANICAL HOUND: FEAR AS A DESIGNED OBJECT
What it is
A machine built in the shape of an animal, kept in the firehouse, that hunts by scent and kills with a needle. It has no will of its own; men set it.
What the society says it is for
Tracking what it is told to track. The firemen bet on it for sport. It is presented as neutral, a tool.
What the reader is meant to ask
If it only reacts to what it is set for, why does it react to Montag? Who set it, and what does it know about him that he does not yet know himself?
Engineered fear works by seeming neutral. Every time the Hound appears, ask who set it and at whom.

📌 CLICK THE MOMENTS IN THE ORDER THEY HAPPEN
Five beats, from the alarm to the flame.

The sentence the woman speaks is real. Hugh Latimer said it, or something very close to it, to his fellow bishop Nicholas Ridley as the two were burned at the stake in Oxford in October 1555 for their beliefs. The novel gives it as: "Play the man, Master Ridley; we shall this day light such a candle, by God's grace, in England, as I trust shall never be put out." (Fahrenheit 451, Part One). Beatty knows the source. Ask yourself what it means that the captain of the book-burners can identify a four-hundred-year-old quotation on sight.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING

Two things happen on Elm Street that the society has no category for: a book speaks, and a fireman's hand disobeys him. Both are turning points, and Bradbury places them within a page of each other.

READING RESPONSE
Copy from your own copy the sentence in which Montag's hand acts on its own in the attic, cited (Part One). Then write a paragraph using the four turning-point questions from Part 1: who is in control at that moment, what the prose slows down for, who speaks, and what Montag carries away. Finish by answering the woman: the Latimer sentence says a candle lit this day will never be put out. In this novel's world, is she right, on the evidence so far? Give one reason from the text. Say whether this scene has moved your answer to the module question, and how.
