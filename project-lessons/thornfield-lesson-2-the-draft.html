Write the lead
The lead has to earn the next paragraph
●
Start from the version you chose in The Workshop. Now write the full lead paragraph, three to five sentences. Then run the stranger test, which means reading it as someone who has never opened the novel. Could that reader follow it without stopping?

Headed sections
Each section holds one idea
●
Your sections come from the plan in The Workshop. Headings should use parallel grammar (the text-features lesson), which means they are built the same way, such as all questions or all starting with a verb ending in -ing. Parallel headings make a page easy to scan.

4
Write one section now
Paraphrase, attribute, cite
●
Never drop a quotation in unexplained. Use the sequence from the governess-question lesson: put the idea in your own words (paraphrase), say who said it (attribute), then cite it.
A MODEL, ON A DIFFERENT TOPIC
Historians of Victorian households note that a large house party could keep dozens of servants working at once (Last Name page #). That means a single weekend of cards and dinners was really a small business run behind the scenes.
●
Notice the order: the idea, then the source in parentheses, then what it means. Now write your first section. Aim for 150 to 250 words.
Your first section, under its heading
5
Pull-quote and sidebar
Two parts that sit beside the main text
●
A pull-quote is one line from the novel that the reader sees before reading the section it comes from. It must be exact, and it must be cited by chapter. Here is how a line from the novel looks as a pull-quote. Yours must come from the angle you chose, so it will be a different line.
A MODEL PULL-QUOTE · CHAPTER 23
"I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will."
Jane Eyre, ch. 23, in MLA form: (Brontë, ch. 23)
Your pull-quote, word for word, with its chapter

Write your sidebar
A short fact box, list, or definition that would slow the main text if it stayed inside.
6
Sources and the source list
At least two, one from JStor, in MLA
●
A source list goes after the feature. It is alphabetized by last name, with a hanging indent (the first line flush left, the later lines indented). Every source you cited gets an entry, and the novel counts as a source.
THE PATTERN, FOR AN ARTICLE FROM JSTOR
Last, First. “Article Title.” Journal Title, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. #–#. JSTOR, URL.
THE PATTERN, FOR A BOOK (A DIFFERENT NOVEL AS A MODEL)
Austen, Jane. Pride and Prejudice. T. Egerton, 1813.
Type your source list
One entry per line, alphabetized, MLA form. Include the novel.

Using Claude on the Draft
Narrow help, then a log line
●
Use Claude the narrow way: ask it to run the stranger test on a paragraph, flag a term you used without defining, or check whether a claim needs a source. Claude never drafts or words a sentence of your feature. Verify any source it names on JStor before you cite it, and add a line to your log saying what you asked, what it said, and what you kept.
Your AI-use log line for the Draft
