What a feature is made of
The parts of the page you will build
●
A feature is not an essay with a title. It has named parts, and a reader scans them before reading a word. Here is the page. You will build every part.

Headline: the title that makes a stranger stop

Standfirst: one sentence under the headline that says what the piece will explain

Byline: your name

Lead: the opening lines. They have to earn the next paragraph

Headed sections: each one explains one idea

Pull-quote: one line from the novel, set apart and cited by chapter

Sidebar: a short fact box that would slow the main text if it stayed inside

Source list: every source you used, in MLA form (the standard way to list sources)

QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Which part gives a stranger the one-sentence promise of what the piece will explain?

Brainstorm
Part B: three ways in
●
A lead has to hook a stranger. Try three different ways in before you pick one.
A MODEL, ON A DIFFERENT TOPIC
Topic: what a Victorian house party like the one at Thornfield in chapters 16 to 18 really involved. A stranger does not know it could last for weeks, kept dozens of servants busy day and night, and existed mostly so families could size up possible marriages in a polite setting.
THREE LEADS FOR THAT TOPIC
(1) A number: how many servants a house party this size needed. (2) A scene: the moment Blanche Ingram enters a room built to show her off. (3) A question: what was a house party actually for? The number surprised me most, so it can anchor the piece.

Three ways to open
Part D: one lead, three sentence shapes
●
The same point can land three ways. You learned these shapes in the periodic-sentence lesson. Look at where the point sits in each.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
A writer wants a short sentence to hit hardest. Where should it go?
THE HOUSE-PARTY LEAD, REWRITTEN THREE WAYS
Periodic: Behind every gown, every card table, and every carefully arranged introduction at a Victorian house party like the one Brontë stages at Thornfield, there stood a small army of servants whose names the guests rarely learned.

Cumulative: A house party like this one required a small army: cooks for a dozen guests and their households, maids to dress hair twice a day, grooms for a stable full of unfamiliar horses, footmen who never sat down.

Short blow: It took an army to stage a courtship.

Feeder 9: Chronicle Audio Minute
Week 7 · 10 points · a first taste of audio before the Quarter 4 podcast
●
A magazine feature is not the only way to explain something to a stranger. Record a single 2 to 3 minute conversational audio segment explaining ONE feature of Jane's world: the same angle you pitched for the Thornfield Chronicle in Week 6 (the real position of a Victorian governess, the Gothic mode and why Brontë uses it, inheritance and money in the novel, or the shape of a coming-of-age story). Talk to a general listener the way you would explain it to a friend who has never read the book. This is a low-stakes warm-up for the produced podcast you will build in Quarter 4; the goal here is one honest, clear take, not a polished production.
YOUR ANGLE (same as your Week 6 Pitch)
  • The real position of a Victorian governess
  • The Gothic mode, and why Brontë uses it
  • Inheritance, marriage law, and money in the novel
  • The shape of a coming-of-age story, and how Jane Eyre fits and bends it
DIRECTIONS
  • Jot a short outline of what you want to say, three or four points at most. This is lightly scripted: know your points, but do not write out and read a full script word for word. It should sound like you talking, not you reading.
  • Record one take, 2 to 3 minutes long, on any device you have (a phone works fine). No editing and no music. If you misspeak, keep going the way you would in a real conversation.
  • Explain your feature to someone who has never read the novel. Assume no prior knowledge; name and briefly explain any term you use.
  • Save your audio file with the correct name, and submit the file itself or a link to it.

CLARITY FOR A STRANGER
Strong: A listener with no prior knowledge of the novel could follow the explanation; terms are defined as they come up. Developing: Assumes knowledge a stranger would not have, or the point is unclear.
4pts
DELIVERY
Strong: Sounds like a real conversation, paced clearly, within the 2 to 3 minute window. Developing: Read word for word from a script, rushed, or outside the time window.
