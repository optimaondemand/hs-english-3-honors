1
What this milestone is
Assemble, ask, write, plan
●
You have captured everything the work needs. Milestone 12 assembles it into one continuous piece for the first time, then asks you to look at it with someone else's eyes and your own, in writing. Captions can still be draft quality. A rough cut is for seeing the whole shape, not for finishing it. Quarter 4 is where you edit, check accuracy a second time, and publish.
The format. Assemble the rough cut as a narrated slide deck, or a short video walkthrough of reference images arranged the way your scenes would be, with captions. The grade is for the pipeline and the thinking.
2
See one peer note
Four questions, four honest answers
WHAT A PEER-FEEDBACK NOTE LOOKS LIKE (A MODEL, NOT YOUR ANSWER)
What did you understand? That the two stations were arguing with each other about whether persuasion or decree carried the ratification debate.
Where were you lost? Between station 2 and station 3 I could not tell whether time had passed or I was still in the same room.
What did the space make you feel? Small, with the press that close in frame. It made the argument feel handmade instead of official.
What would you cut? The third title card repeats what the narration already says. I would drop one of them.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED

Brainstorm before you ask
One sentence
●
Name, in one sentence, the moment you are least sure an outside viewer will understand. That is what you listen for hardest when your peer answers the four questions. A viewer's honest confusion there is worth more than a compliment anywhere else.

At least one peer, all four answers
●
Edit your captured material from Milestones 10 and 11 into one continuous piece at full length, in order. Then have at least one peer watch the whole rough cut once and answer four questions in a written note. Paste or type their answers here, in their words.

First name and last initial.
5
Draft, reflect, plan
Three pieces of writing
●
Draft your director's statement. It is your interpretation of the unit's contested question, defended from your sources. Then write a reflective memo of about 300 words naming what the medium did to two specific film-grammar choices you made. Close with a revision plan for Quarter 4: what you will fix, and in what order.
Your director's statement draft
The contested question, your position, how specific choices render it, and the strongest alternative reading. Draft quality is fine.
Your reflective memo, about 300 words
Name two film-grammar choices. For each: what you planned, and what the medium did to it.
Your revision plan for Quarter 4
What you will fix, in order. Use your peer's note and your own viewing.
6
Get flags on the statement draft
Flags only, never rewrites
●
Use Claude, then log it. Paste your director's statement DRAFT to Claude and ask it to flag only two things: any unsupported claim, and any missing alternative reading. Claude never writes or rewrites the statement. It only flags. Treat every flag as a question. Check it against your own sources, revise the statement yourself, and record the outcome.
What did you ask Claude, in your own words?
Write what you actually pasted or typed, in your own words.
What did Claude flag: unsupported claims, and missing alternative readings?
How did you check each flag against your own sources?
Name the source you opened. Claude points. You decide.
What did you change, and what did you keep even though it was flagged?
What did you revise in the statement yourself?
Your AI-use log line, in one to three sentences
What you asked, what it flagged, what you verified and changed. This line goes in your submission.
