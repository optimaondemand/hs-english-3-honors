What an episode is made of
Four segments, in this order
●
An episode is a short radio show. You are the host of an underground broadcast called The Living Books. In the novel, the people who resist keep books alive by memorizing them. Your episode does a version of that on air: it saves one work or idea, and it argues that the work deserves saving.
●
The cold open is a short hook that makes a stranger keep listening. The recitation is the passage you learned by heart and speak with your eyes covered. The case is your argument for why the work must survive. The close is your sign-off, your credits, and your sources read aloud.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Which segment is the part where you argue that the work must survive?

4
Reflect: why must it survive?
Part A of the Workshop
●
Start with the work or idea you pitched in Week 2. It can be a public-domain text you have met this year, a passage from any book you own, or a passage from the novel itself. First, study how one writer reflects. The example below saves a different work from anything you could pick, so copying its shape will not hand you your own episode.
A WORKED EXAMPLE (A SHORT PUBLIC-DOMAIN SPEECH ABOUT CIVIC COURAGE)
This speech must survive because it names a specific fear plainly, in public, at real risk to the speaker. Most listeners have only heard it quoted in fragments that lose its argument. My listener has heard its most famous line online but has never heard the case it was built to make.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED

Why must this work survive?
Be specific. What would actually be lost if no one ever met it again?
Who is listening?
Picture one real kind of listener. What do they already know? What do they not know?

Brainstorm: three ways in
Part B of the Workshop
●
An episode needs a cold open that hooks a listener in the first thirty seconds. Try three different ways in before you pick one. The writer of the worked example tried a surprising fact, the single line everyone half-knows, and a question. The surprising fact won because a listener can check it and it is hard to forget.
Hook 1: a surprising fact
Hook 2: a question
Hook 3: a moment or a scene
Which one fact or line will anchor the whole episode, and why?
Choose the one a listener can check and will remember.

Sources and credits
Part C continued
●
Your episode needs at least two credible sources. At least one must come from JStor, an online library of journal articles and books. Cite them in MLA style, the citation format you have used all year. Then list any music or sound. It must be royalty-free, which means free to use under its license, or made by you. A sound you record yourself, like a page turning, is always allowed.

8
Stylize: open your case three ways
Part D of the Workshop
●
A listener hears your case once and cannot reread it. How you shape the first line decides whether they stay. Try three shapes. You met fragments in the lesson on the fragment and the simile, and marking a script in the lesson on producing an episode. This is the same skill, used on your own opening.
A WORKED EXAMPLE: ONE LONG SENTENCE
If you think a speech this old could not possibly still be dangerous to hear, consider that the very city where it was delivered refused, for years afterward, to let it be printed again.
A WORKED EXAMPLE: A FRAGMENT SEQUENCE
Banned from reprint. For years. In its own city. That is not a museum piece. That is a live wire.
A WORKED EXAMPLE: A QUESTION
Why would a city ban its own speech from being printed again, decades after it was given?

9
The censor's counter-broadcast
Part E of the Workshop: test your idea
●
A case that has never faced its strongest objection is only a case nobody has tested. In the novel, a fire captain argues against books. You will borrow his method on yourself, on purpose, as a test. You ask Claude, an AI assistant, to build the strongest case against saving your work, as if it wrote for a rival broadcast that wants it forgotten. You do not accept the case. You check it, and then you answer it on air, in your own words.
1
Ask
Tell Claude what you are saving. Ask for the strongest case against it.
2
Check
Verify every factual claim against a real source.
3
Answer
Write your on-air answer in your own words.
4
Log
Record what you asked, what Claude said, what you checked, what you decided.
Claude never writes your case or any line of your script. It can produce a confident claim that turns out to be wrong, so nothing goes on air until you have checked it yourself.
A MODEL EXCHANGE, ON A DIFFERENT WORK
The student asked: give me the strongest case that a modern audience does not need to hear a short speech from 1852. Claude gave a case that the speech's legal conditions no longer exist, and it cited a later court ruling as proof that the complaint was settled. The student checked the ruling and found it covered a narrower question than Claude claimed. The on-air answer named that gap, then argued that a text can name an injustice long after the specific fight is over.
