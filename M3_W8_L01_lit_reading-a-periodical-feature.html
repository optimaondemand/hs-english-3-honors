📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"The cry has gone forth." A Victorian magazine writer opens an essay on governesses with nine words that make you keep reading. You are about to write one of these yourself.
You finished Jane Eyre. Now you become a writer for a literary-cultural periodical, explaining one part of Jane's world to a reader who has never opened the book. Before you draft a word, you need to know how a feature is actually built: what a lead does, what a heading promises, what a pull-quote is for, and where research goes. Today's reading tool is a labeled anatomy of a feature page. To ground it, read real sentences from an actual Victorian periodical, the kind of publication a feature imitates.

A feature page has a shape, and the shape is not an accident

An informative feature is a piece of nonfiction writing built for a reader who is not required to read it. Nobody has assigned your magazine's readers this article; they will only keep reading if the page earns it, one part at a time. Every part below has one job. Learn the job, and the draft follows.

THE ANATOMY OF A FEATURE PAGE
HEADLINE
A short title that names the subject and hints at the angle. Not a full sentence; not a summary of everything below it.
STANDFIRST
One sentence under the headline that tells a stranger what they are about to learn and why it matters right now.
LEAD (the opening paragraph)
Earns the second paragraph. Often a scene, a striking fact, or a direct address to the reader, never a dry announcement of the topic.
SECTION HEADINGS
Break the explanation into one idea per section, so a reader can see the whole shape at a glance and find a part again later.
PULL-QUOTE
A striking line pulled out of the text and set apart, larger, to draw a skimming reader in and give them something to remember.
SIDEBAR
A short box beside the main text for a fact that is useful but would slow the main argument down if it stayed inside it.
SOURCE LIST
Where the claims came from, in MLA form, so a skeptical reader can check the work.
Every part above answers the same test: does this help a stranger, or does it only prove I did research?
📖 PART 2 · A REAL VICTORIAN PERIODICAL PIECE

A feature imitates a real kind of writing, so read a real example of it: the anonymous essay "Hints on the Modern Governess System," Fraser's Magazine, vol. 30 (November 1844), pp. 571–583, an English monthly of exactly the kind Brontë herself wrote for and was reviewed in. It is public domain and scanned at the Internet Archive; the excerpts below are quoted directly from that scan, cited by page. Watch for: where does the writer address you, the reader, directly? Where does an idea get restated in plainer words right after a hard one?

📖 Read: "Hints on the Modern Governess System" (excerpts · public domain, Internet Archive)

Opening (p. 571): "The cry has gone forth. The plaint in the drawing-room has been echoed by groans from the work-room. All reflecting womankind, from Lady Emmeline to the milliner's apprentice who wrote touchingly to Lord Ashley, make their moan."

On why the position exists at all (p. 572): "Let them ponder deeply the governess system of the present day. This was the watch-word, 'Teach us on our own terms, or work, and cease to be gentlewomen.'"

On who the piece holds responsible (p. 573): "The modern governess system is a case between woman and woman. Before one sex demands its due from the other, let it be just to itself."

On the governess's isolation inside a full house (pp. 574–575): "It is only the governess, and a certain class of private tutors, who must hear the echoes from the drawing-room and the offices, feeling that, in a house full of people, they dwell alone."

On old age with no pension (p. 575): "What is to become of those women who, after toiling out the best of their days, survive to old age? How are they to subsist?… the superannuated governess has not even these."

Stable source: Internet Archive, Fraser's Magazine, November 1844 (vol. 30, no. 179): archive.org/details/sim_frasers-magazine_1844-11_30_179.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

The Fraser's writer above earned a Victorian reader's attention the same way any writer has to earn a reader's: by naming the trouble plainly, then explaining it without ever losing the person on the other side of the page.
