📖 WHY THE CHRONICLE NEEDS SOURCES
A general reader who has never opened Jane Eyre has no reason to trust a feature that only asserts things. What earns that trust is a feature that shows its work: real sources, checked, cited, and used honestly. Your Thornfield Chronicle piece explains one part of Jane's world to that reader, and it needs at least one source from JStor to back it up. You already learned the full JStor workflow in Module 2. This page is a refresher, not a new lesson, pointed at the Chronicle's research.
❓ YOUR QUESTION AND YOUR PLAN, BEFORE YOU SEARCH
A search without a question returns everything and settles nothing. Before you open JStor, write three short lines at the top of your Chronicle notes. Bring all three to the Week 7 project lesson; a teacher may ask to see them.
1
The question. One sentence, phrased as a question a stranger could answer with evidence, not a topic. "The governess" is a topic. "Why did a governess in the 1840s rank below the family but above the servants, and what did that cost her?" is a question.
2
The plan. Two places to look (JStor is one; the novel itself, a period source, or a reference work can be the other) and one line on what kind of source would actually settle your question: a historian's article, a document from the period, a scholar's reading of the novel.
3
The revision, after your first search. Rewrite the question narrower or aimed differently, using what the first results taught you. Under it, one honest line on what did not work (terms too broad, results all about the wrong decade, nothing scholarly) and what you changed. A question that survives its first search unchanged is usually too vague to have been tested.

1. Sign in. Open the Canvas-native "Research & JStor Access" page inside the Start Here module and sign in at www.jstor.org with the credentials posted there. Those credentials live only on that page; never write them anywhere else, including here.
2. Search. Use two or three key terms pulled from your chosen Chronicle angle, not a full sentence.
3. Filter to scholarly. In the results sidebar, limit the content type to journal articles, where peer review lives.
4. Skim an abstract. Read the short summary at the top of an article before opening the whole thing; it tells you in a minute whether the source actually addresses your angle.
5. Evaluate credibility. Run the C.R.A.P. check from Module 2: Currency (when was it written), Relevance (does it address your actual question), Authority (who wrote it and where), Purpose (is it informing, arguing, or advocating).
6. Save the citation and export MLA. On the article page, use the "Cite" option, choose MLA, and paste the citation into your working document the moment you decide to use the source.

READING RESPONSE
Run one search on JStor for your chosen Chronicle angle. Write down your search terms, and the titles, authors, and publication years of two promising articles from your results. You are logging what you found, not claiming you have read or used them yet; that comes later, when you write the Chronicle draft.
🤖 WORKING WITH AI

Claude can suggest search terms for your angle and summarize an abstract you paste in. It cannot tell you whether an article exists or what it says: AI tools routinely invent titles, authors, and journals that do not exist. Only cite a source you found and opened on JStor yourself; every citation in your Chronicle is verified on JStor itself.
