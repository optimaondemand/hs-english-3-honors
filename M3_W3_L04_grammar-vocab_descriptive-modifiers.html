📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"Basking in the light and heat of a superb fire, lay Pilot… Half reclined on a couch appeared Mr. Rochester, his foot supported by the cushion." Two kinds of modifier, doing two different jobs, in back-to-back sentences.
Brontë layers description onto a sentence without piling up new clauses, using two related tools: the participial phrase, which modifies a specific noun, and the absolute phrase, which modifies the whole scene at once. Today you learn to tell them apart in her own Gothic description, then Word Study: cachinnation and the borrowed French phrase tête-à-tête, both from chapters 11 through 15.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a participial phrase from an absolute phrase in Brontë's own sentences and explain what each one modifies.
2
I will be able to write one sentence of my own using a participial phrase and one using an absolute phrase, each labeled.
3
I will be able to use cachinnation and tête-à-tête precisely, and explain tête-à-tête's literal French meaning.
✏️ PART 1 · TWO WAYS TO LAYER A DESCRIPTION

A participial phrase is built from an -ing or -ed verb form and modifies one specific noun in the sentence, usually the subject. A absolute phrase is different: it pairs a noun with a participle, but it has no verb of its own linking them, and instead of modifying one word it modifies the whole scene, adding a detail alongside the main action rather than inside it. The test for a participial phrase: ask which noun it describes, and place it near that noun. The test for an absolute phrase: it can be lifted out of the sentence whole, noun and participle together, and the main clause still stands complete without it; it is commenting on the moment, not attached to any single word in the main clause.

TWO MODIFIERS, ONE FIRESIDE SCENE (CHAPTER 13)
Two wax candles stood lighted on the table, and two on the mantelpiece; basking in the light and heat of a superb fire, lay Pilot—Adèle knelt near him. Half reclined on a couch appeared Mr. Rochester, his foot supported by the cushion. (Jane Eyre, ch. 13)
Participial phrase
"Basking in the light and heat of a superb fire" modifies one noun: Pilot, the dog lying by the hearth. It could move next to "Pilot" without changing its job.
Absolute phrase
"His foot supported by the cushion" is a noun ("foot") plus a participle ("supported"), with no linking verb. It adds a detail to the whole scene of Rochester on the couch, not to one word in the main clause.
Both let Brontë add a visual layer without starting a new sentence. The difference is what each phrase is attached to.

📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapters 12 and 14)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
cachinnation
noun		 Jane, weighing whether to be afraid of the laugh in the corridor: "but that neither scene nor season favoured fear, I should have been superstitiously afraid" and, just before it, "no circumstance of ghostliness accompanied the curious cachinnation." (Jane Eyre, ch. 12) Loud, immoderate laughter, usually with a harsh or unsettling edge rather than genuine mirth. It is a rare, formal word, and Brontë reaches for it at the exact moment she wants the sound of the laugh to feel clinical and strange rather than simply "a laugh." Notice that the sentence pointedly withholds "ghostliness" from the word: the narrator is arguing herself out of superstition even as she names the sound with the strangest available word for it. From Latin cachinnare, "to laugh loudly," itself likely echoic, an imitation of the sound of harsh laughter, related to similar noise-imitating words across several old languages.
tête-à-tête
noun phrase, borrowed from French		 Rochester explaining why he does not enjoy children's company: "It would be intolerable to me to pass a whole evening _tête-à-tête_ with a brat." (Jane Eyre, ch. 14) A private, face-to-face conversation between two people, usually implying some intimacy or seriousness. English borrowed the phrase whole from French and still italicizes it in Brontë's text, marking it as foreign even to a Victorian reader. Rochester's usage is half a joke: he uses an intimate, adult phrase to describe an evening with a child, which is itself part of the joke. French, literally "head to head" (tête, "head," repeated with à, "to"). The image is of two people leaning their heads close together to talk privately, which English kept as the phrase's entire meaning.
📓
WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For cachinnation, write a sentence describing a real or invented sound, choosing this word specifically because a plainer word ("laughter") would lose its harsh, formal edge; note in one line what the plainer word would lose. For tête-à-tête, write a sentence using the phrase correctly as a noun (for example, "a tête-à-tête over coffee"), and then translate its two French words literally. Notice how many French phrases entered English exactly this way, kept whole rather than translated, because no single English word captured the same picture.
