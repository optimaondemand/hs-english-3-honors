📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A play cites act, scene, and line. A novel has no such fixed address, so you cite by chapter, and you learn to fold a quotation into your own sentence so smoothly a reader barely feels the seam.
Writing about a novel requires citing Jane Eyre correctly and blending its quotations into your own prose. Today you learn the Works Cited entry for a novel (shown two ways, since the edition you read may differ from a print copy), in-text citation by chapter, signal phrases, brackets, and ellipses, all taught on real sentences from this module.

✏️ PART 1 · CITING A NOVEL WITHOUT STABLE PAGES

A play has act, scene, and line. A poem has stanza and line. A novel you read as an ebook has no page number that means anything to a classmate reading a different edition. MLA solves this by citing a novel in-text by chapter instead of page, and by building the Works Cited entry from whichever edition you actually used. Both moves below are correct; use the one that matches your source.

WORKS CITED: TWO CORRECT ENTRIES FOR THE SAME NOVEL
The Project Gutenberg ebook (the edition embedded in this module)
Bronte, Charlotte. Jane Eyre. Project Gutenberg, 2008, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1260.
A print edition (confirm the exact publisher and year your copy uses; a scholarly critical edition or a standard trade paperback both follow this same pattern)
Bronte, Charlotte. Jane Eyre. [Publisher], [Year of this edition].
Fill the brackets with the exact information on your own copy's title and copyright pages; do not guess a publisher or date you cannot see.
IN-TEXT: SIGNAL PHRASE, BRACKETS, AND ELLIPSIS, ON REAL SENTENCES FROM THIS MODULE
CITE BY CHAPTER, NOT PAGE
Jane tells St. John plainly, "I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will" (Bronte, ch. 23).
No page number appears because different editions paginate differently; the chapter number is stable across every copy of the novel.
SIGNAL PHRASE (names the speaker so the quotation is not a floating fragment)
When St. John frames marriage as an obligation rather than a feeling, Jane answers him directly: "I scorn your idea of love" (ch. 34).
The signal phrase, "Jane answers him directly," tells the reader who is speaking and why before the quotation begins.
BRACKETS (a small clarifying insertion inside a quotation)
Jane insists the inheritance be split "shared equally" among "the nephew and three nieces of our uncle [John Eyre]" (ch. 33).
The bracketed name clarifies who "our uncle" is for a reader who has not read the chapter; Bronte's own words are not otherwise touched.
ELLIPSIS (marks real, verified omitted text)
Jane tells St. John, "I scorn your idea of love ... I scorn the counterfeit sentiment you offer" (ch. 34), cutting the clause "I could not help saying, as I rose up and stood before him, leaning my back against the rock" from the middle of the original sentence.
Use an ellipsis only over text you have actually checked against the source and cut on purpose, never to disguise a misquotation. Always verify the cut against the original chapter.

📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapters 36–38)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
conflagration
noun		 The innkeeper explains why letters to Thornfield went unanswered: "The grim blackness of the stones told by what fate the Hall had fallen…by conflagration." (Jane Eyre, ch. 36) A large, destructive fire. The word is more formal and more total than "fire" alone; it names a blaze that consumes a whole structure, not a contained or minor one. Notice the innkeeper reaches for this heavier word exactly when he is describing the scale of the loss. From Latin conflagrare, "to burn up completely," from com- ("together, thoroughly") plus flagrare ("to blaze, to burn"). The same root flagrare gives English "flagrant," originally "blazingly obvious."
lachrymose
adjective		 Jane, hearing Rochester's dependence on her voice: "The water stood in my eyes to hear this avowal of his dependence…But I would not be lachrymose: I dashed off the salt drops." (Jane Eyre, ch. 37) Tearful, or given to weeping. Jane names the state she refuses to fall into even as she describes the tears actually forming; the word marks the exact moment she chooses composure over display, a small echo of the self-command she has practiced since Gateshead. From Latin lacrima, "tear" (the kind that falls from an eye). The same root gives the anatomical term "lacrimal," for the glands that produce tears.
