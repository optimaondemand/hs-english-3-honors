📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You know this novel so well you can forget your reader does not. Today's whole lesson is remembering them.
Every essay you have written for this course so far has had one reader: a teacher who already knows the text and is checking your thinking. An informative feature flips that. Your reader has never opened Jane Eyre, does not owe you their attention, and will stop reading the moment you lose them. Today you learn the "explain it to a stranger" test, why a feature holds one idea per section, why a term gets defined the moment it first appears, and why the voice you have used all year has to change.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to apply the "explain it to a stranger" test to a paragraph of my own writing.
2
I will be able to organize an explanation into sections that each carry exactly one idea.
3
I will be able to define a term the first time I use it and avoid the private tone of an essay written only for a teacher.
👤 PART 1 · THE STRANGER TEST
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture explaining your favorite book to a friend who has never read it and is only half paying attention. What do you do differently than you would writing for your teacher?

The stranger test is simple: before you keep a sentence, ask whether a real person who has never heard of Jane Eyre, Brontë, or Thornfield could follow it without stopping. If the answer is no, the sentence needs a definition, a reorder, or a cut, not a footnote. Apply the test to every section, not just the opening; a feature can lose a reader in paragraph six just as easily as paragraph one.

📋 PART 2 · ONE IDEA PER SECTION

A section that tries to do two jobs does neither well. Before you write a section heading, finish this sentence in your head: "This section explains ___, and only that." If you cannot finish it with one noun phrase, the section is actually two sections wearing one heading.

TWO IDEAS CROWDED IN ONE SECTION
Heading: "Governesses and the Gothic." One paragraph explains what a governess earned; the next explains what a locked attic means in a Gothic novel. A reader arrives looking for one thing and gets two half-finished ones.
ONE IDEA, CLEARLY HELD
Heading: "What a Governess Actually Earned." Every sentence under it serves that one question. The Gothic material gets its own heading and its own section, later, fully finished.
💬 PART 3 · DEFINE ON FIRST USE, AND LOSE THE ESSAY VOICE

Two habits separate a feature from a school essay. First, define a term the moment you use it, in the same sentence or the next one, briefly: not "the Gothic mode" alone, but "the Gothic mode, a style built on hidden rooms, family secrets, and dread." Second, drop the essay voice, the "this essay will argue" and "as evidenced by" phrasing built for a reader who is grading you. A feature writer talks to a reader who could put the magazine down.

📝 A MODEL PARAGRAPH, WRITTEN FOR THIS LESSON

The paragraph below is not a published text. It was written for this lesson to show the stranger test passing, sentence by sentence: term defined on first use, one idea held, no essay voice.

A governess in a novel like Jane Eyre occupied a strange middle rank: too educated to eat with the servants, too poorly paid to be treated as an equal by the family that employed her. Charlotte Brontë makes this discomfort visible in a single scene at a house party, where the guests discuss governesses as a class while one sits, unacknowledged, in the same room.
✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING
📓
READING RESPONSE
Take the lead paragraph you wrote in the periodical-feature lesson and run the stranger test on it sentence by sentence. For each sentence, write "pass" or "needs work," and for anything that needs work, revise it on the spot. Then write the feature's working list of section headings, each one a single noun phrase naming one idea, in the order a stranger would need them.
🤖 WORKING WITH AI

Claude can read a paragraph and tell you honestly whether a term goes undefined or a section seems to hold two ideas instead of one; that is a genuinely useful stranger-test check. It cannot decide which idea belongs in which section for you, and it should never write the sentence that fixes the problem; that revision is yours.
