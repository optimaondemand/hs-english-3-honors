📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Montag calls in sick. His captain visits anyway, sits on the bed, and talks for a very long time. Nobody argues back.
Captain Beatty is the novel's most dangerous character, and he never once raises his voice or a weapon. He argues. Today's reading tool is a way of listening to a censor's argument and hearing its moving parts instead of just its conclusion: the claim he wants you to accept, the flattering premise that makes the claim easy to accept, and the false choice he offers instead of a real one. Beatty tells Montag a history of how the firemen came to be, and the history is really a case: that people themselves, not any government, asked for speed, simplicity, and the removal of anything that could offend. You read his visit the way you would read a lawyer's opening statement, watching for what he is asking you to grant for free.

📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone told you a rule was for your own good, and you were not sure you believed them. What made you suspicious, or what put you at ease?

Reading a censor's argument

A censor rarely says "obey me." A skillful one argues, and the argument usually has the same three parts. First, a claim: some version of "this was done for you, and you already wanted it." Second, a flattering premise, an unproven assumption about the audience that is pleasant enough that no one questions it: that people are simple, that people want ease more than truth, that conflict is the only real harm. Third, a false choice, where the only two options offered are total agreement or total chaos, with the middle ground, actual disagreement handled like an adult, quietly removed from the menu. Once you can name these three moves, a speech that sounds reasonable on first hearing starts to show its seams.

THREE MOVES OF A CENSOR'S ARGUMENT
1. The claim
"This was done for you, and you asked for it." Responsibility moves from the censor to the audience before the argument even starts.
2. The flattering premise
An assumption about people that feels good to accept: you deserve ease, you deserve to feel certain, difficulty is unkindness.
3. The false choice
Only two doors are shown: total agreement, or total conflict. The real, ordinary middle, people disagreeing and living with it, is never on offer.
As you read Beatty's visit, mark where you hear each move. He uses all three, more than once.
📖 PART 2 · NOW READ: BEATTY'S VISIT

Continue in Part One from Montag's return home the night of the Elm Street burning: his unrest in bed, his decision the next morning to stay home sick, and Captain Beatty's visit to the house. Read through Beatty's whole account of how the firemen came to be and his defense of a society built for speed and comfort, ending when he leaves the house. Watch for: where Beatty says the change began with the public itself, not with any office or law; how he describes a book as a kind of weapon; and the single sentence in which he reduces every hard question to a choice between one side and none.

📖 Read: Fahrenheit 451, Part One, from Montag's return home the night of the Elm Street fire through the end of Captain Beatty's visit. In your own copy.
🤝 PART 3 · NOW YOU: TRACE THE HISTORY BEATTY GIVES

Put these five stages of Beatty's history in the order he gives them. Each is described in your own words, not quoted. Use what you read.

📌 CLICK THE STAGES IN THE ORDER BEATTY GIVES THEM
Five stages, from faster entertainment to firemen with a new job.

Two lines from Beatty's own account are worth holding onto. On what a book represents: "A book is a loaded gun in the house next door." (Fahrenheit 451, Part One). And on the false choice he offers instead of an actual argument: "If you don't want a man unhappy politically, don't give him two sides to a question to worry him; give him one. Better yet, give him none." (Fahrenheit 451, Part One). Read the second line twice. It moves from one side to none in a single breath, and Beatty says the word none as though it were the generous option.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING

Beatty never once tells Montag to stop thinking. He tells Montag a story in which Montag already agreed to stop, a long time ago, along with everyone else.

Then answer in a paragraph: where in his history does Beatty put the blame on the public rather than on any fireman or official, and what does he gain by putting it there?
