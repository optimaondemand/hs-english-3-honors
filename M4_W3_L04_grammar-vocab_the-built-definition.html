📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Faber does not describe books. He defines them, the way a scientist defines a term, and then keeps extending the comparison until it explains an entire argument.
Faber's teaching in Part Two uses two precise tools that any writer can borrow. The first is the built definition: term, class, distinguishing feature, the same three-part structure a dictionary uses, but aimed at winning a point instead of just informing. The second is the extended metaphor: a single comparison, stated once and then developed, detail by detail, across several sentences, the way you already saw the sieve and the sand extended across Part Two's opening. Today you build both, using model sentences and this lesson's own definitions as your examples. Then Word Study: two words tied directly to Faber's teaching and to this module's own method of quoting exactly, dictum and verbatim.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a definition with all three parts: term, class, and distinguishing feature.
2
I will be able to extend a single metaphor across several sentences instead of using it once and dropping it.
3
I will be able to use dictum and verbatim precisely in sentences of my own.
✏️ PART 1 · TWO TOOLS FOR MAKING AN IDEA STICK

A built definition has three parts. The term is the word being defined. The class places it inside a broader category it belongs to. The distinguishing feature says exactly what separates it from every other member of that class. "A sieve is a kitchen tool [class] that lets fine material pass through a mesh while holding back anything coarser [distinguishing feature]." Skip the class and a definition floats without context; skip the distinguishing feature and it could describe a dozen other things.

An extended metaphor takes one comparison and keeps building on it, adding a new matching detail every time it returns, instead of trading it for a new comparison each time. The test of an extended metaphor is not how clever the first line is; it is whether the second and third appearances add something the first one did not already say.

THE BUILT DEFINITION: THREE PARTS, WORKED FROM TODAY'S OWN WORDS
These two definitions are this lesson's own Word Study words, built correctly, so you can see the tool and learn the words at the same time.
DICTUM · term + class + distinguishing feature
A dictum is a formal, authoritative statement, one made with the weight of settled judgment behind it, as though the matter it addresses is already closed.
Term: dictum. Class: a formal, authoritative statement. Distinguishing feature: delivered as though the matter is already closed, not open for debate.
VERBATIM · term + class + distinguishing feature
To quote something verbatim is to reproduce someone's exact words, with nothing added, removed, or rephrased, down to the punctuation.
Term: verbatim. Class: a way of reproducing someone's exact words. Distinguishing feature: nothing added, removed, or rephrased, down to the punctuation.
Notice that this whole module's quotation bank is itself an act of quoting verbatim: every Bradbury line on these pages is reproduced exactly as the bank gives it, because the novel's copyright makes anything less than exact unacceptable to print.

📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Both words below are tied to Faber's manner of teaching and to this module's own method of quoting exactly, rather than to a single word printed in the novel. The "In the text" column names where each idea applies.

WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
dictum
noun		 Part Two: the manner in which Faber delivers his account of the three things a book holds, not as an opinion offered for debate but as a settled judgment, spoken plainly, the moment he decides to trust Montag enough to say it. A formal, authoritative statement, delivered as though the matter is already settled. Applied to Faber, it captures how flatly he speaks once he commits to helping Montag, in contrast to his earlier, more hesitant hedging. From Latin dictum, "a thing said," the neuter past participle of dicere, "to say, to speak." The same root gives English dictate, diction, and verdict (literally, "a true saying").
verbatim
adverb / adjective		 This module's own method: every Bradbury line printed anywhere in Module 4 is reproduced verbatim from the approved quotation bank, because the novel's copyright leaves no room for an approximate or paraphrased quotation dressed up in quotation marks. Faber's own insistence on precision, never settling for a loose gloss of a text, is the same standard applied to a person instead of a policy. Word for word; reproduced exactly, with nothing added, removed, or rephrased. The opposite of a summary or a paraphrase. From Latin verbum, "word," with the adverbial ending -atim: literally, "word by word."
