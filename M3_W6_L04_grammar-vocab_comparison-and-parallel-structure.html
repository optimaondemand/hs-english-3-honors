📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"Far better that crows and ravens should pick my flesh from my bones, than that they should be prisoned in a workhouse coffin." A sentence built to weigh one thing against another, and hold both up at once.
This week Jane weighs one road against another, again and again: death on the moor against a pauper's grave, a proposal from one man against a proposal from another, love against duty. The sentence tool for weighing two things is comparison and parallel structure: matching grammatical parts (phrase against phrase, clause against clause) so a reader can hold both sides in the same glance. Today you learn the three moves Brontë uses to do this, and you build your own from real sentences in chapters 28 through 35.

✏️ PART 1 · THREE WAYS TO WEIGH TWO THINGS IN ONE SENTENCE

When a sentence sets two things side by side, the grammar has to carry the comparison, not just the words. Match the parts (noun against noun, phrase against phrase, clause against clause) and the reader can weigh both sides cleanly. Break the match and the comparison goes lopsided, even if every word is spelled right. Three moves do most of the work: the direct comparative ("rather X than Y"), the correction frame ("not X, but Y"), and full parallel structure across two roles or situations ("as X… but as Y…"). Comparatives and superlatives (better, far better, worse, hardest) are the small words that signal a comparison is coming; they only work if what follows is actually parallel.

THREE WAYS TO WEIGH TWO THINGS, IN BRONTË'S OWN SENTENCES (CHAPTERS 28–35)
THE DIRECT COMPARATIVE · "rather X than Y," "better X than Y"
far better that crows and ravens—if any ravens there be in these regions—should pick my flesh from my bones, than that they should be prisoned in a workhouse coffin and moulder in a pauper's grave. (Jane Eyre, ch. 28)
Two fates, both given as a full "that…" clause, so the reader can set one directly against the other. The comparative word better promises a second clause is coming; the parallel that…should… pattern delivers it.
THE CORRECTION FRAME · "not X, but Y"
You shall be mine: I claim you—not for my pleasure, but for my Sovereign's service. (St. John, Jane Eyre, ch. 34)
Two matched prepositional phrases, for my pleasure and for my Sovereign's service, so the correction lands cleanly: the reader is told what is not true and then, in the same shape, what is.
FULL PARALLEL STRUCTURE · "as X… but as Y…"
As his curate, his comrade, all would be right: I would cross oceans with him in that capacity… but as his wife—at his side always, and always restrained, and always checked…this would be unendurable. (Jane Eyre, ch. 34)
Two imagined roles, as his curate and as his wife, each opening its half of the sentence in the identical grammatical slot. The parallel makes the two lives easy to compare and the gap between them impossible to miss.
Test the sentence: underline the two things being weighed. If they are not the same part of speech, doing the same grammatical job, the comparison will not hold its balance. Fix the grammar and the meaning gets sharper for free.

📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapters 31 and 34)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
inexorable
adjective		 Watching St. John refuse Rosamond Oliver out of duty: "Diana Rivers had designated her brother 'inexorable as death.' She had not exaggerated." (Jane Eyre, ch. 31) Impossible to move by pleading, argument, or feeling; fixed on a course no appeal can change. The word is almost always used of a person or a force that will not bend, no matter how much it is asked to. Notice that Brontë reaches for a comparison to say it, "as death," because ordinary description will not convey how total the refusal is. From Latin inexorabilis: in- ("not") plus exorare ("to prevail upon by entreaty," itself from ex- and orare, "to pray, to plead"). Literally, a person who cannot be reached by prayer or pleading.
despotism
noun		 Jane, realizing St. John is not as far above her as she had believed: "I saw his fallibilities: I comprehended them…The veil fell from his hardness and despotism." (Jane Eyre, ch. 34) The exercise of absolute, unchecked power over another person, without regard for their consent. Jane uses it here not of a nation but of a habit of mind: St. John's way of directing everyone around him as though his judgment alone should decide. Pairing it with hardness shows the word carrying real moral weight, not a mild criticism. From Greek despotes, "master, lord, absolute ruler," by way of French despotisme. The Greek word originally described the head of a household with total authority over its members before it came to describe a ruler with total authority over a state.

WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For each word, write one new sentence of your own on a subject unrelated to the novel, keeping the meaning exact. For inexorable, describe a force, deadline, or person that truly cannot be moved, not just one that is merely stubborn; use a comparison ("inexorable as ___") the way Brontë does. For despotism, show the unchecked power in action rather than simply naming it. Then a copia move: rewrite the ch. 34 despotism sentence using a milder synonym in its place, and note in one line what the sentence loses.
💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Explanation is full of comparison: what this was like versus what a reader might assume, what one option cost versus another. These three moves keep a comparison clean, one of the fastest ways to make an unfamiliar fact land.
