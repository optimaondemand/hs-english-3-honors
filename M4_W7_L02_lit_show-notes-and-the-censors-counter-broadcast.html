📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Beatty argued that people asked to have their books taken away. Your broadcast preserves one thing worth saving. Today you have to build the strongest argument against your own choice, before anyone else can use it against you.
Two things today. First, show notes: the written companion to your episode that a listener reads alongside it, listing your sources in MLA and a plain summary of what is inside. Second, and central to this module, the censor's counter-broadcast: you ask Claude to build the strongest possible case against preserving the work or idea your episode defends, exactly the way Beatty built a case against books in Part One. You do not accept that case. You verify it, and then you answer it on air, in the segment called "the case."
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a set of show notes with a summary, segment list, and MLA source list.
2
I will be able to request, verify, and answer a counter-argument against my own argument, with sources checked.
3
I will be able to tell a weak answer to an objection from a strong one.
📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
If you had to argue against the very thing you have spent five weeks preparing to defend, what would be the strongest, most honest objection someone could raise?

Reading tool: the censor's method, reused as a rigor test

Back in Week 2 you studied how Beatty argues: he claims people wanted their books removed, and he offers a single, simplified reason for a complicated problem. Today you use that same method on yourself, deliberately, as a test of rigor rather than an attack. A case for preservation that has never had to answer its strongest objection is not actually a strong case; it has just never been tested. The censor's counter-broadcast is how you test it, on your own terms, before anyone else does.

📋 PART 2 · YOUR SHOW NOTES

Show notes are the written page that accompanies an audio episode: a listener who wants more than the recording reads them alongside it. Yours need three parts. A summary, two or three sentences, telling a stranger what the episode preserves and why. A segment list, naming each of the four segments (cold open, the read passage, the case, close) with a rough timing for each. And a source list in MLA, covering at least two credible sources, including at least one from JStor, for every factual claim your episode makes. Nothing in your episode should assert a fact your show notes cannot trace back to a source.

Summary
What the episode preserves and why it matters, in a sentence or two a stranger could understand with no other context.
Segment list
Cold open, read passage, the case, close, each with a rough timing, so a listener can navigate the episode.
Source list (MLA)
At least two credible sources, at least one from JStor, covering every factual claim in the episode.
🤖 PART 3 · THE CENSOR'S COUNTER-BROADCAST

This is the module's one integrated Claude step, and it works in four parts. One, tell Claude what your episode preserves and ask it to give you the strongest, most serious case against preserving it, as if Claude were writing for a rival broadcast that wants it forgotten. Two, read the case Claude gives you and check every factual claim it makes against a real source; Claude can produce a confident-sounding claim that turns out to be wrong or unsupported, so nothing in the counter-broadcast may go on air unverified. Three, decide, in your own words, how to answer the case; Claude never writes your response for you. Four, log the whole exchange: what you asked, what Claude said, what you verified, and what your answer became.

A MODEL EXCHANGE (written for this lesson; your own topic will differ)
STUDENT ASKED
"My episode preserves a short abolitionist speech from 1852. Give me the strongest case that a modern audience does not need to hear it anymore."
CLAUDE GAVE (a claim requiring verification)
A case arguing the speech's specific legal and political conditions no longer exist, so its urgency belongs to its own moment rather than to now, and citing a specific later court ruling as evidence that the speech's central legal complaint was fully resolved.
STUDENT VERIFIED, THEN ANSWERED
The student checked the cited ruling against a real source and found it addressed a narrower question than Claude implied. The on-air answer named that gap directly, then argued that a text's power to name an injustice clearly outlasts the specific legal fight it was written for, which is why the speech is still taught and quoted today.

AI-use log line: record, in your own words, what you asked Claude, what it produced, what you personally verified and how, and what your final on-air answer became.

🤖 WORKING WITH AI

This lesson teaches the censor's counter-broadcast step. Claude may generate a confident, well-argued objection that contains a claim you cannot verify. Treat every factual claim it makes as unverified until you check it yourself, and never let an unverified claim reach your script.
