📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"A time to keep silence, and a time to speak." Ecclesiastes 3 says it in eight words. Most writers would need a paragraph. Today you learn to write like that on purpose.
Every sentence in the two passages you read this week does a full amount of work in very few words. That is concision: saying exactly what is needed and nothing more, out of respect for the person who has to sit and listen. It matters this week for a second reason. You are about to write a document whose entire purpose is concision: a résumé, or professional summary, which compresses a whole self into one page. Today you learn the grammar that makes a page like that work, and then you draft one.

1
I will be able to explain why concision is a form of respect for a reader's or listener's time.
2
I will be able to apply the conventions of a résumé or professional summary to compress a self onto one page.
3
I will be able to use vanity and travail precisely in sentences of my own.
✏️ PART 1 · CONCISION AS A GRAMMAR OF RESPECT
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of the longest, most padded set of instructions you have ever had to sit through. What did the padding cost you as a listener?

Concision is not the same thing as brevity for its own sake. A concise sentence is not short because the writer ran out of things to say; it is short because the writer has already cut away everything the idea did not need. Ecclesiastes 3, read earlier this week, is a model of the habit: fourteen paired opposites, each one only a few words long, and the chapter never once explains itself further than that. The writer trusted the reader to supply the rest. Concision, done well, is a form of respect: it assumes your listener is capable, and it does not waste the limited attention they are giving you.

PADDED VS. CONCISE: THE SAME IDEA, TWICE
Both versions below were written for this lesson; they are not from either passage.
PADDED
In my personal opinion, I think that it is fairly important, in a general sense, for a person to try to keep in mind, whenever they can, that there is basically a kind of appropriate moment for pretty much every different type of action a person might take.
CONCISE
There is a season for every action.
Notice what the concise version cut: hedges ("in my personal opinion," "fairly," "in a general sense," "basically," "pretty much"), throat-clearing ("I think that"), and doubled words ("kind of," "type of"). Nothing true was lost. The idea arrived faster, and the reader was trusted to receive it whole.

Two edits, every time you draft: cut the hedge (a word that softens a claim you actually believe, like "sort of" or "I feel like"), and cut the double (two words doing one job, like "basic fundamentals" or "future plans"). Read the sentence aloud after each cut; if the meaning is untouched, the cut was correct.

📋 PART 2 · THE COUSIN GENRE: THE RÉSUMÉ AS A ONE-PAGE SELF

A résumé or professional summary is concision applied to an entire life. It compresses years of experience, education, and skill onto a single page, and it does so by conventions that are worth learning explicitly, because they show up almost nowhere else in school writing.

Headings, not paragraphs
Experience, Education, Skills. A reader should be able to scan the page in seconds and land exactly where they need to.
Parallel verb phrases
Every line under a heading starts the same grammatical way: past-tense action verbs (organized, led, built), never a mix of tenses or a mix of nouns and verbs in the same list.
No first person
Never "I organized the fundraiser." Just "Organized the fundraiser." The subject is implied, and dropping it is a rule of the genre, not an error.
Dates, precise and consistent
Month and year, formatted the same way throughout, right-aligned so a reader's eye can track a timeline without hunting for it.

The résumé is a useful cousin to a spoken broadcast script for a specific reason. Both genres compress a great deal into a small, fixed space, and both succeed or fail on the same test: does every remaining word earn its place, or is it there out of habit.

✍️ PART 3 · DRAFT A ONE-PAGE RÉSUMÉ

Draft a one-page résumé or professional summary, choosing one of two subjects: yourself, honestly, using your real activities and interests, or a character from any text you have read this year (Montag, Faber, Granger, Hamlet, Jane, or another), invented plausibly from what the text shows about them. Use headings, parallel past-tense verb phrases, no first person, and consistent dates.

📓
READING RESPONSE
Draft your one-page résumé or professional summary (yourself or a character, your choice), with at least three headings and at least two parallel-verb lines under each. Then run the two-edit test from Part 1 on your own opening summary line: circle any hedge or double, cut it, and write the before and after side by side.
📕 PART 4 · WORD STUDY (from Ecclesiastes 3 and Revelation 22)

Both words this week come from the two public-domain passages read earlier this week; they may be quoted directly.

WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
vanity
noun		 “so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity.” (Ecclesiastes 3:19) Emptiness; a lack of lasting substance or value. Not primarily "conceit" here (a later, narrower sense); the King James sense is closer to "a breath, a vapor, something that cannot be held onto." From Latin vanitas, "emptiness, worthlessness," from vanus, "empty, hollow." The King James translators reached for it to render a Hebrew word (hevel) that literally means "vapor" or "breath," the same word that gives Ecclesiastes its refrain.
travail
noun		 Ecclesiastes 3:10, in the King James Version embedded in the earlier lesson on Ecclesiastes and Revelation: “I have seen the travail, which God hath given to the sons of men to be exercised in it.” (Ecclesiastes 3:10) Hard, wearing labor; toil that costs the body and the spirit, and in older English the labor of childbirth as well. In Ecclesiastes the word names the work God has set before every person, offered not as a curse but as the thing that exercises us. It is the plain word for what the book people take on when they become books: a burden carried on purpose. From Old French travail, “work, toil, hardship,” from a Late Latin verb tripaliare, “to torture,” itself from trepalium, a three-staked frame used as an instrument of torture. The English word travel is the same word: a journey was once, by definition, an ordeal.
📓
WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For vanity, write one sentence of your own that uses the King James sense (emptiness, not conceit); for travail, write one sentence using the "remain, endure" sense and a second sentence using the "tolerate" sense, so you can feel how the same word covers both.
