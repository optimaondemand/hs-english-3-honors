THE MODULE 3 RECITATION
Choose a Poem, Learn It by Heart, and Speak It
Read this page in Week 1 so you know what is coming. This quarter's passage is a poem, not a prose speech: a Victorian-era poem chosen from the menu below. Two of the seven are sung inside Jane Eyre itself; the others were written by Brontë's contemporaries and share the novel's questions about endurance, faith, and a self that will not be governed from outside. You choose in Week 4, after the poetry lesson, rehearse in Week 5, and record or perform in Week 6.
🎤 WHAT THIS IS

Once each quarter you memorize and perform a passage connected to that quarter's text. In Quarter 1 it was a speech from Hamlet; in Quarter 2, a passage of Douglass's oratory. This quarter it is verse. A poem learned by heart is different from a speech learned by heart: the line breaks tell you where to breathe, the rhymes tell you where the thought turns, and the meter is a pulse you carry in your body. That is why the Week 4 lesson on reading a Victorian poem comes before you choose. When a poem is inside you, you understand it differently, and you keep it for life.

🎯 HOW YOU PERFORM IT (READ CAREFULLY)

You recite from memory, with your eyes covered. Wear a blindfold, close your eyes, or turn away from any screen or page. This proves the words are truly memorized and frees you to perform rather than read. There are two ways to do it, and you choose one:

🎥 Option A: Record a video
Record yourself on any device you have (a phone is fine), eyes covered, then submit the video on the Week 6 assignment page. Include a caption or transcript of the poem so your teacher can check accuracy.
💬 Option B: Live with your teacher
Your teacher may instead choose to meet with you and hear the recitation live, also with eyes closed. If so, they will schedule it; you do not submit a video.
📜 CHOOSE ONE POEM (15–37 LINES) · TAP TO PREVIEW

Every poem below is in the public domain, so the full text is here for you. Read all seven aloud once before you decide; pick the one your voice keeps returning to. Where a span is marked, you may recite that continuous span instead of the whole poem.

The ballad Bessie sings · Jane Eyre, chapter 3 (Charlotte Brontë, 1847)
Whole poem, 20 lines in five stanzas. You meet it in your Week 1 reading.
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
My feet they are sore, and my limbs they are weary;
Long is the way, and the mountains are wild;
Soon will the twilight close moonless and dreary
Over the path of the poor orphan child.

Why did they send me so far and so lonely,
Up where the moors spread and grey rocks are piled?
Men are hard-hearted, and kind angels only
Watch o’er the steps of a poor orphan child.

Yet distant and soft the night breeze is blowing,
Clouds there are none, and clear stars beam mild,
God, in His mercy, protection is showing,
Comfort and hope to the poor orphan child.

Ev’n should I fall o’er the broken bridge passing,
Or stray in the marshes, by false lights beguiled,
Still will my Father, with promise and blessing,
Take to His bosom the poor orphan child.

There is a thought that for strength should avail me,
Though both of shelter and kindred despoiled;
Heaven is a home, and a rest will not fail me;
God is a friend to the poor orphan child.

A nursemaid's ballad that the ten-year-old Jane hears as "the saddest cadence of a funeral hymn." Choose this if you want a passage from inside the novel with a refrain to anchor your memory. It rewards a plain, unhurried voice that lets the refrain change meaning each time it returns.

A song sung in chapter 24 · Jane Eyre (Charlotte Brontë, 1847)
The first six stanzas, 24 lines, from "The truest love that ever heart" to "Disasters gather nigh." You reach this chapter in Week 5; the text is here so you can plan ahead, without its context.
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
The truest love that ever heart
Felt at its kindled core,
Did through each vein, in quickened start,
The tide of being pour.

Her coming was my hope each day,
Her parting was my pain;
The chance that did her steps delay
Was ice in every vein.

I dreamed it would be nameless bliss,
As I loved, loved to be;
And to this object did I press
As blind as eagerly.

But wide as pathless was the space
That lay our lives between,
And dangerous as the foamy race
Of ocean-surges green.

And haunted as a robber-path
Through wilderness or wood;
For Might and Right, and Woe and Wrath,
Between our spirits stood.

I dangers dared; I hindrance scorned;
I omens did defy:
Whatever menaced, harassed, warned,
I passed impetuous by.

A love song in short, driving stanzas, sung by one character to another. Choose this for its momentum: the lines are brief and the rhymes close, so it sits quickly in the memory and asks for a voice that can build. Wait until Week 5 to decide what the singer means by it.

"Life" · Charlotte Brontë, from Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell (1846)
Whole poem, 24 lines. Published the year before Jane Eyre, under the same pen name.
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
Life, believe, is not a dream
So dark as sages say;
Oft a little morning rain
Foretells a pleasant day.
Sometimes there are clouds of gloom,
But these are transient all;
If the shower will make the roses bloom,
O why lament its fall?
Rapidly, merrily,
Life’s sunny hours flit by,
Gratefully, cheerily
Enjoy them as they fly!
What though Death at times steps in,
And calls our Best away?
What though sorrow seems to win,
O’er hope, a heavy sway?
Yet Hope again elastic springs,
Unconquered, though she fell;
Still buoyant are her golden wings,
Still strong to bear us well.
Manfully, fearlessly,
The day of trial bear,
For gloriously, victoriously,
Can courage quell despair!

Brontë's own voice, a year before Jane's. Choose this if you want a poem that changes pace on purpose: the quick tripping lines ("Rapidly, merrily") against the steadier ones. It rewards a speaker who can hear the shift in rhythm and let the voice follow it.

"No Coward Soul Is Mine" · Emily Brontë (written 1846, published 1850)
Whole poem, 28 lines. Charlotte called these "the last lines my sister Emily ever wrote."
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
No coward soul is mine,
No trembler in the world’s storm-troubled sphere:
I see Heaven’s glories shine,
And faith shines equal, arming me from fear.

O God within my breast,
Almighty, ever-present Deity!
Life—that in me has rest,
As I—undying Life—have power in Thee!

Vain are the thousand creeds
That move men’s hearts: unutterably vain;
Worthless as withered weeds,
Or idle froth amid the boundless main,

To waken doubt in one
Holding so fast by Thine infinity;
So surely anchored on
The stedfast rock of immortality.

With wide-embracing love
Thy spirit animates eternal years,
Pervades and broods above,
Changes, sustains, dissolves, creates, and rears.

Though earth and man were gone,
And suns and universes ceased to be,
And Thou were left alone,
Every existence would exist in Thee.

There is not room for Death,
Nor atom that his might could render void:
Thou—Thou art Being and Breath,
And what Thou art may never be destroyed.

Jane's creator's sister, refusing fear. Choose this for its declarative force: every stanza plants a flag. It rewards a low, steady voice that grows without hurrying, and it is the menu's most demanding poem to hold in the memory because its stanzas are so alike in shape.

"Dover Beach" · Matthew Arnold (1867)
Whole poem, 37 lines; or the continuous span from "The sea of faith" to the end (23 lines).
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
The sea is calm to-night.
The tide is full, the moon lies fair
Upon the straits; on the French coast, the light
Gleams and is gone; the cliffs of England stand,
Glimmering and vast, out in the tranquil bay.
Come to the window, sweet is the night-air!
Only, from the long line of spray
Where the sea meets the moon-blanched sand,
Listen! you hear the grating roar
Of pebbles which the waves draw back, and fling,
At their return, up the high strand,
Begin and cease, and then again begin,
With tremulous cadence slow, and bring
The eternal note of sadness in.

Sophocles long ago
Heard it on the Ægean, and it brought
Into his mind the turbid ebb and flow
Of human misery: we
Find also in the sound a thought,
Hearing it by this distant northern sea.

The sea of faith
Was once, too, at the full, and round earth’s shore
Lay like the folds of a bright girdle furled.
But now I only hear
Its melancholy, long, withdrawing roar,
Retreating, to the breath
Of the night-wind, down the vast edges drear
And naked shingles of the world.

Ah, love, let us be true
To one another! for the world, which seems
To lie before us like a land of dreams,
So various, so beautiful, so new,
Hath really neither joy, nor love, nor light,
Nor certitude, nor peace, nor help for pain;
And we are here as on a darkling plain
Swept with confused alarms of struggle and flight,
Where ignorant armies clash by night.

A man at a window, speaking to someone he loves, about a world that has lost its certainties. Choose this for its long, irregular lines and its one great turn ("Ah, love, let us be true"). It rewards patience: the pauses are the performance. This poem returns next quarter, in a very different setting.

"Invictus" · William Ernest Henley (written 1875, published 1888)
Whole poem, 16 lines. The shortest choice, and the hardest to make sound new.
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
Out of the night that covers me,
Black as the Pit from pole to pole,
I thank whatever gods may be
For my unconquerable soul.

In the fell clutch of circumstance
I have not winced nor cried aloud.
Under the bludgeonings of chance
My head is bloody, but unbowed.

Beyond this place of wrath and tears
Looms but the Horror of the shade,
And yet the menace of the years
Finds, and shall find, me unafraid.

It matters not how strait the gate,
How charged with punishments the scroll,
I am the master of my fate:
I am the captain of my soul.

Written by a young man in a hospital bed after losing a leg. Choose this if you want the year's question stated as plainly as it can be: a self that governs itself. Because so many people know the last two lines, the challenge is to earn them, stanza by stanza, so that they arrive as a conclusion and not a slogan.

"Ulysses," the closing lines · Alfred Tennyson (1842)
The continuous span from "There lies the port" to the last line, 26 lines. The whole poem is 70 lines; the full text is on Wikisource if you want the context.
📖 Read the full poem (public domain)
There lies the port: the vessel puffs her sail:
There gloom the dark broad seas. My mariners,
Souls that have toil’d, and wrought, and thought with me—
That ever with a frolic welcome took
The thunder and the sunshine, and opposed
Free hearts, free foreheads—you and I are old;
Old age hath yet his honour and his toil;
Death closes all: but something ere the end,
Some work of noble note, may yet be done,
Not unbecoming men that strove with Gods.
The lights begin to twinkle from the rocks:
The long day wanes: the slow moon climbs: the deep
Moans round with many voices. Come, my friends,
’Tis not too late to seek a newer world.
Push off, and sitting well in order smite
The sounding furrows; for my purpose holds
To sail beyond the sunset, and the baths
Of all the western stars, until I die.
It may be that the gulfs will wash us down:
It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles,
And see the great Achilles, whom we knew.
Tho’ much is taken, much abides; and tho’
We are not now that strength which in old days
Moved earth and heaven, that which we are, we are;
One equal temper of heroic hearts,
Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will
To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

An old king talking his sailors into one last voyage. Choose this for its unrhymed lines that run past their endings; you must find the breath-points yourself, the way you did with Shakespeare in Quarter 1. It rewards a voice that can be tired and unbeaten at once.

You choose in Week 4, after the poetry lesson. If you are unsure, read all seven aloud once and pick the one your voice returns to.
📊 HOW IT IS GRADED (20 POINTS)
Criteria Strong Developing Pts
Accuracy of text
7 pts		 From memory, eyes covered, faithful to the words and the line order; slips are rare and self-recovered. Frequent errors, skipped stanzas, or long stalls; or eyes open / clearly reading. 7
Vocal delivery
7 pts		 Clear and controlled; pace, pause, and stress follow the sense, not just the line ends; the meter is felt without being pounded. Rushed or flat; a sing-song beat that ignores meaning; pauses only at line breaks; hard to follow. 7
Interpretation
6 pts		 Delivery choices show real understanding of the poem's turn and its weight. Recited correctly but with little sense of what the words mean or where the poem turns. 6
Total: 20 pts
