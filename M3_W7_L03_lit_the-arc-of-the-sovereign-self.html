📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A ten-year-old shut in a room cries "Unjust!" A grown woman at Ferndean chooses a blind man freely. Between those two moments, the same self says no, five separate times, to five separate people who wanted her to be less than whole.
You have now read the whole novel. Today's lesson steps back from any single scene to trace the shape of Jane's arc across all thirty-eight chapters, then sets that arc beside the other selves this year has studied: a prince who examined his own conscience before acting, a pseudonymous arguer defending a form of government, a philosopher urging self-trust, and a man who claimed his own selfhood against literal bondage. This is the lesson where the whole year's question comes into view at once.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to place Jane's five key refusals in the order they occur and explain what each one costs her.
2
I will be able to connect Jane's arc to at least two other selves studied this year, naming a real point of comparison for each.
3
I will be able to state my own current answer to the module question, using Jane's arc as evidence.
📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN

Reading tool: the arc as a labeled timeline

A character's arc becomes visible only when you step back far enough to see the whole shape at once, the way you might step back from a single brushstroke to see a painting. Jane's arc is built from five moments where she is offered a version of safety, love, or belonging that requires her to become less than a whole person, and she says no every time. Read the timeline below left to right. At each point, notice not just what she refuses, but what refusing costs her right then, since the cost is different every time.

THE ARC: FIVE REFUSALS, GATESHEAD TO FERNDEAN
CH. 2 · GATESHEAD
"Unjust!"
A child refuses to accept a punishment she has not earned. Cost: the red-room itself.
→
CH. 4 · GATESHEAD
"I am not deceitful"
A child refuses a false label and hands it back to the person who gave it to her. Cost: the after-taste of vengeance.
→
CH. 23 · THORNFIELD
"A free human being"
A young woman refuses to stay for love without a lawful marriage. Cost: the man she loves.
→
CH. 27 · THORNFIELD
"I care for myself"
She refuses to become Rochester's mistress even in her lowest, loneliest moment. Cost: a home and every certainty she had.
→
CH. 34 · MOOR HOUSE
"I scorn your idea of love"
A grown woman refuses duty without love. Cost: a great purpose and her cousin's regard.
Five refusals, five different costs, one self that will not shrink to fit anyone else's terms.
📖 PART 2 · THE YEAR'S OTHER VOICES

Jane is not the only self this year has studied under pressure. Set her arc beside the others, briefly, to see what the comparison reveals.

Hamlet (Module 1)
A prince examines his own conscience before he will act, delaying rather than accepting a role handed to him from outside. Jane's refusals are faster, but the same insistence on examining a demand before yielding to it runs through both.
Publius (Module 2)
A pseudonymous author argues that a government of laws, not of unchecked will, protects a people from tyranny. St. John's proposal in chapter 34 asks Jane to accept his will as her law; her refusal is a one-person version of the same argument.
Emerson (Module 2)
"Trust thyself" is Emerson's whole argument compressed into two words. Every one of Jane's five refusals is an act of trusting her own judgment over an authority, a suitor, or a cousin who is certain he knows better.
Douglass (Module 2)
Douglass claims his own selfhood against literal, legal bondage, a claim of an entirely different order of cost than Jane's. Setting the two side by side is not a claim that their situations were equal; it shows how far the idea of a self that will not be owned reaches across this year's very different texts.

READING RESPONSE · YOUR CLAIM
Write the first draft of your end-of-module answer to the question: What does it cost to stay true to one's conscience under the pressures of love, faith, and authority, and is it worth it? Use at least three of Jane's five refusals as evidence, naming the specific cost of each, and bring in at least one of the year's other voices (Hamlet, Publius, Emerson, or Douglass) as a point of comparison. This is a first draft, not a final essay: aim for a real, defensible position, not a summary of the novel.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Every text this year has asked the same underlying question in a different costume: what does a self owe itself when the world outside demands something less than the whole of it? Whatever angle a writer takes, a governess's real position, the Gothic mode, inheritance and marriage law, or the shape of a coming-of-age story, the work explains one more piece of the world that tested this same sovereign self.
