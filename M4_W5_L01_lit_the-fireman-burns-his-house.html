📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Beatty does not arrest Montag. He hands him a flamethrower and orders him to burn his own house, room by room, with his own hands.
Part Three opens with the alarm from last week arriving at Montag's own front door. Beatty already knows, or has guessed enough, and instead of a simple arrest he designs a punishment that is also a test: Montag will destroy everything he owns himself, in front of the crew, in front of Mildred, before anything else happens to him. Today's reading tool asks you to read the act itself, not just the plot event, as an argument. Every forced destruction says something and costs something, and the two are rarely the same thing.

📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
If someone wanted to punish you in a way that was also a message to everyone watching, would forcing you to destroy something of your own be a harsher punishment than simply taking it from you? Why or why not?

Reading an act as an argument

A forced act can still say something, and what it says is rarely identical to what it costs. To read a scene like this one, separate three questions. What does the act claim, on its surface, to the people watching it (here: that the system still works, that even a suspected fireman will do his duty, that nothing has really changed)? What does the act actually cost the person performing it (here: every object in the house, and something harder to name, that does not show up in an inventory)? And where do the two split apart, the moment the claim and the cost stop matching? A punishment designed to look like obedience can, underneath, be the last step of an argument the character has already been making with himself for weeks.

THE CLAIM, THE COST, AND THE SPLIT
What the act claims
The system still works. Duty outranks any private doubt. Punishment restores order.
What the act costs
Every object Montag owns, room by room, and a marriage that has already been unraveling for weeks.
Where they split
Read for the exact moment obedience stops looking like obedience, and start asking what Montag is actually doing by the last room.
