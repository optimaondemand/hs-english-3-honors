📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A novel gives you paragraphs. A poem gives you lines, and the line is where the poet decides exactly how much of a thought you get at once.
You have been reading Brontë's prose closely all module: sentence shape, dialogue, description. A poem asks something a little different of you. Today's lesson gives you five tools, stanza, line and line break, speaker versus poet, meter felt as a pulse, and the sound devices a poet reaches for, using Victorian poems as the examples. By the end of the lesson you choose the poem you would make your own.

Five tools for reading a poem

A stanza is a group of lines set off by white space, a poem's version of a paragraph, though a stanza is usually built on a repeating shape rather than a single idea. A line is one row of the poem as the poet set it on the page, and the line break, where one line ends and the next begins, is a choice: it can close a thought (end-stopped) or cut across one, forcing you to carry the sense over the gap (enjambment). The speaker is the voice inside the poem, and the poet is the person who wrote it; they are sometimes close and sometimes not the same at all, and a careful reader checks rather than assumes. Meter is a pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables that gives a line a pulse you can feel if you read it aloud and tap the stresses; you do not need to name every foot, only to feel the beat return. And a handful of sound devices do most of the remaining work: alliteration (repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words), assonance (repeated vowel sounds inside nearby words), rhyme scheme (the pattern of end-rhymes, labeled with letters, A, B, A, B and so on), and refrain (a line or phrase that returns unchanged, usually at the same point in each stanza).

STANZA ANATOMY, LABELED · "INVICTUS," W. E. HENLEY (1875), STANZA 1
ONE STANZA
Out of the night that covers me,
line 1 · rhyme A
Black as the Pit from pole to pole,
line 2 · rhyme B
I thank whatever gods may be
line 3 · rhyme A
For my unconquerable soul.
line 4 · rhyme B
Four lines, a rhyme scheme of ABAB, and each line end-stopped: the sense finishes where the line does. That is a stanza doing exactly what a stanza does.
FEEL THE PULSE · ONE LINE, ONE TIME
i THANK whatEVer GODS may BE
Read it aloud, tapping on the gold syllables. Four soft-then-hard beats in a row: that steady rise-and-fall is the line's meter. You do not need the Greek name for it. You only need to feel that the beat returns, evenly, four times, and that this is part of why the line is easy to remember.

Now the other three sound devices, each from a different Victorian poem.

ALLITERATION
“Worthless as withered weeds”
Emily Brontë, “No Coward Soul Is Mine” (1846)
Three w sounds in a row make the line itself sound worn out, which is the point: the poet is dismissing something as trash.
ASSONANCE
“Where the sea meets the moon-blanched sand”
Matthew Arnold, “Dover Beach” (1867)
The short a sound in “blanched” and “sand” ties the two words together by ear, even though they do not rhyme.
REFRAIN
“…the poor orphan child”
Bessie's ballad, Jane Eyre, ch. 3
The same four words end all five stanzas. The words never change; what changes is how much you have learned about the child by the time you hear them again.
📖 PART 2 · SPEAKER, POET, AND THE LINE BREAK

Two more things to notice before you choose. First, speaker versus poet: the song in chapter 24 of the novel is sung by a character, in a scene, for a reason of his own; the singer is not Brontë. "No Coward Soul Is Mine" is different: Charlotte Brontë herself introduced it as "the last lines my sister Emily ever wrote," so most readers hear Emily Brontë's own voice close behind the speaker. Always ask who is talking before you decide what a poem believes. Second, the line break as a choice: read this from "Dover Beach," where the sentence keeps running past the end of each line: "Upon the straits; on the French coast, the light / Gleams and is gone; the cliffs of England stand, / Glimmering and vast, out in the tranquil bay." (Matthew Arnold, “Dover Beach,” 1867). The grammar does not stop where the line does; Arnold is choosing to make you carry the thought across the gap. That carrying-over is called enjambment, and it feels different from a line that closes its own thought, the way each line of the "Invictus" stanza above does.

✍️ PART 4 · CHOOSE A POEM

The five tools apply to any poem. Read each poem quoted in this lesson aloud once. Notice where your own voice wants to land: on a poem's rhyme, its refrain, its pulse, or its long unrhymed reach. That reaction is real evidence, not a distraction from the decision.
