📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"My name is Briggs, a solicitor of —— Street, London." One clause names him; the next, without a verb of its own, tells you exactly what he is for.
This week's chapters are full of people being named and, in the same breath, defined: a lawyer, an uncle, a bride, a wife. Today you learn the appositive, a noun phrase set beside another noun phrase to rename or define it, and you practice the definition move it makes possible: naming something and explaining what it is, in one economical sentence. ✏️ PART 1 · THE APPOSITIVE AS A DEFINITION MOVE

An appositive is a noun phrase placed next to another noun phrase to rename or define it, with no verb of its own connecting the two: "Briggs, a solicitor," "Bertha Mason, the true daughter of an infamous mother." Set off the appositive with commas when the information adds detail without narrowing down which specific person or thing you mean (Brontë only has one Briggs); skip the commas when the appositive is doing the narrowing itself, the way a title before a name behaves ("my brother Rowland," where Rochester has more than one sibling to distinguish). The move is efficient: instead of a second sentence ("Briggs was a solicitor. He practiced on a London street."), the definition rides along inside the first sentence, at no extra grammatical cost. Writers reach for it constantly when a new person enters a scene and needs placing fast.

THE APPOSITIVE, ANATOMIZED · IN BRONTË'S OWN SENTENCES
NAMING A STRANGER FAST
My name is Briggs, a solicitor of —— Street, London. (Jane Eyre, ch. 26)
Main clause names him; the appositive phrase tells you his job and place, both essential facts, in three words.
A DEFINITION THAT CARRIES A JUDGMENT
Bertha Mason, the true daughter of an infamous mother, dragged me through all the hideous and degrading agonies… (Jane Eyre, ch. 27)
The appositive here does not just add a fact; it delivers Rochester's own harsh judgment about inherited character, in the same breath as the naming.
A DEFINITION THAT TURNS BITTERLY IRONIC
In the shape of Miss Ingram; a noble and beautiful woman,—your bride. (Jane Eyre, ch. 23)
Jane's own appositive chain, stacking descriptor on descriptor; she means every word of it as an accusation, not a compliment, at the moment she says it.
One more, brief and plain: "her uncle, Mr. Gibson" (ch. 22). Not every appositive carries a judgment; sometimes it is just the fastest way to say who someone is.

READING RESPONSE
Write three sentences each naming a real person you know (a friend, a teacher, a family member) with an appositive that defines them. (1) A plain, factual appositive, the way "her uncle, Mr. Gibson" simply clarifies a relationship. (2) An appositive that carries a real judgment or opinion, the way Rochester's appositive for Bertha does, used fairly and kindly about someone you respect. (3) An appositive chain of two or three descriptive phrases, the way Jane's does for Miss Ingram, though yours should build admiration rather than accusation. Punctuate all three correctly with commas.
📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapter 27)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
avaricious
adjective		 Explaining why his father arranged his first marriage, Rochester asks Jane: "And did you ever hear that my father was an avaricious, grasping man?" (Jane Eyre, ch. 27) Greedy for money or wealth, in a way that shapes a person's whole character and decisions. Notice Brontë pairs it with grasping, a near-synonym; the doubling makes the accusation land harder than either word alone, and sets up the whole explanation that follows: a father who would arrange his son's marriage purely for a fortune. From Latin avarus, "greedy, covetous," related to avere, "to crave, to long for." The English suffix -icious (compare malicious, capricious) marks a settled, characteristic quality, not a single greedy act.
profligate
noun / adjective		 Rochester imagines how Jane must see him: "you must regard me as a plotting profligate—a base and low rake who has been simulating disinterested love in order to draw you into a snare." (Jane Eyre, ch. 27) A person of shamelessly immoral or wasteful habits, someone who has abandoned self-restraint entirely. As a noun it names the person; as an adjective it describes the behavior ("profligate spending," "a profligate life"). Rochester uses it about himself, hypothetically, to test how harshly Jane is judging him, which tells you as much about his own guilt as it does about the word's meaning. From Latin profligare, "to cast down, to ruin, to destroy," from pro- ("forward, down") and fligere ("to strike"). The sense shifted from "ruined" to "given to ruinous, dissolute living."
📓
WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For each word, write one new sentence of your own on a subject unrelated to the novel, keeping the meaning exact. For avaricious, make sure your sentence shows greed shaping a decision or a character, not just wanting something once. For profligate, use it about behavior or a lifestyle, not a single mistake; the word implies a pattern, not an incident. Then a copia move: rewrite Rochester's profligate sentence with a plainer synonym in its place, and note in one line what the plainer word loses.
💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Explaining real historical facts and figures to readers who have never met them calls for appositives: one of the fastest, cleanest tools for introducing someone new without a second sentence every time.
