📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
This module asks you to quote more carefully than any before it: the Apology, verbatim, and your own copy of the novel, verbatim. Today you learn the mechanics that make a careful quotation actually readable.
You have already been asked to quote fairly this module: the woman on Elm Street, Socrates, the poem in the parlor. Today you learn the mechanics behind that habit: how to punctuate a quotation inside a quotation, when a quotation is long enough to need its own block, how to cut a long quotation honestly with an ellipsis, how to insert a clarifying word with brackets, and how to avoid the one failure that turns fair quoting into Beatty's kind of weapon: pulling a phrase out of the context that gave it its meaning.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to punctuate a quotation inside a quotation correctly, with double marks outside and single marks inside.
2
I will be able to set a long quotation off as a block, and cut and clarify a quotation honestly with ellipses and brackets.
3
I will be able to use gadfly and reproach precisely, and explain what each meant in Socrates's own mouth.
✏️ PART 1 · NESTED QUOTATION MARKS

American convention uses double quotation marks for a quoted sentence. When the material you are quoting already contains a quotation inside it, that inner quotation converts to single quotation marks, so a reader can tell at a glance which words belong to whom.

A MODEL SENTENCE (written for this lesson, not from any assigned text)
The reporter wrote, “The witness told the court, ‘I never saw him again after that night,’ before changing her account an hour later.”
Double marks hold the whole quoted sentence; single marks hold the witness's own words inside it. If you needed a third layer, a quotation inside that quotation inside this one, you would return to double marks again, though that depth is rarely needed.

You can practice this rule on real material. In Excerpt A1 from the Socrates lesson, Socrates recalls what he says to any Athenian he meets: he tells them they are “not ashamed of heaping up the greatest amount of money and honour and reputation.” If you wanted to quote a sentence that itself quotes Socrates quoting himself this way, the outer sentence would use double marks and Socrates's own words would drop to single marks inside them, exactly like the model above.

✏️ PART 2 · BLOCK QUOTATION, ELLIPSIS, AND BRACKETS

MLA style sets a long quotation, four lines or more of prose, off as a block quotation: indented, without quotation marks (the indent itself signals that every word is quoted), introduced by a sentence and a colon. Excerpt A2 from the Socrates lesson, the gadfly passage, is long enough that quoting all of it in your own writing would require a block, not a quotation mark pair inside a sentence.

A BLOCK QUOTATION, CORRECTLY SET (from Excerpt A2)
I am that gadfly which God has attached to the state, and all day long and in all places am always fastening upon you, arousing and persuading and reproaching you. You will not easily find another like me, and therefore I would advise you to spare me.
No quotation marks surround the block; the indent does that work instead. A sentence before it (something like: Socrates makes his most famous comparison in the gadfly passage:) introduces it, and the citation follows the block, not inside it.

Two more tools finish the toolkit. An ellipsis (three spaced periods, …) marks material you have honestly cut from the middle of a quotation because it was not needed for your point, never because it disagreed with you. Brackets let you insert a word the quotation needs to make sense outside its original sentence, usually a name in place of a pronoun.

ELLIPSIS AND BRACKETS, APPLIED TO EXCERPT B
Original: “I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living”
Honestly shortened: “[Socrates] say[s] again that daily to discourse about virtue… is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living.”
The bracketed word replaces the pronoun "I" so the sentence makes sense outside Socrates's own mouth; the ellipsis cuts a clause without changing what the surviving words mean.
⚖️ PART 3 · THE ETHICS OF QUOTING FAIRLY

Every tool above can also be used dishonestly. An ellipsis can cut the one clause that would have reversed a quotation's meaning. A bracket can insert a word the original speaker never intended. And even a perfectly accurate short quotation can mislead if it is pulled out of the argument that gave it its point, which is exactly what Beatty does in the quotation duel when he fires quoted lines meant to contradict each other rather than build toward anything. Quoting fairly means keeping enough context that a reader who has not read the source could still trust your quotation, and never cutting or bracketing your way around a phrase's real meaning to win a point it was not making.

📕 PART 4 · WORD STUDY (from the Apology)

The Apology is public domain, so both words are quoted directly from the excerpts in the Socrates lesson.

WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
gadfly
noun		 Excerpt A2: “I am that gadfly which God has attached to the state, and all day long and in all places am always fastening upon you, arousing and persuading and reproaching you.” A biting fly that pesters livestock into motion; used of a person who provokes others, usually by uncomfortable questioning, into examining themselves or acting when they would rather stay still. From Middle English gad ("goad, spike") plus fly; the literal insect gave its name to anyone who "goads" others the way the insect goads cattle.
reproach
verb / noun		 Excerpt A1: “if I think that he has no virtue in him, but only says that he has, I reproach him with undervaluing the greater, and overvaluing the less.” To express disapproval or disappointment to someone directly, holding their own words or actions up against a standard they claimed to meet; sharper than criticism, gentler than condemnation. From Old French reprochier, itself likely from Latin re- ("back") and prope ("near"): literally, to bring something back near enough that a person cannot avoid facing it.

WORD STUDY · TRY IT
Copy the exact sentence from Excerpt A1 or A2 for each word. Then write one new sentence of your own for each, on a subject unrelated to the novel or the Apology, keeping the meaning exact. For gadfly, use it for a person, not an insect, the way Socrates does. For reproach, aim it at a real gap between what someone claimed and what they did, not at a simple mistake.
