📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A script reads perfectly silent and trips the moment it is spoken aloud. Your own recording is the only proofreader that catches that.
You listened yesterday, to a peer's work and your own draft. Today you act on what you heard. A rough recording teaches something a script on the page cannot: where a sentence that looked fine on paper runs out of breath, where a claim needed a pause it never got, and where the writing was working harder than the voice needed it to. This lesson gives you three specific revision moves to carry your draft into a final, and a chance to put them in the order that actually works.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a rough recording reveals that a written script alone cannot.
2

3

🎤 PART 1 · WHAT A ROUGH RECORDING TEACHES THAT A SCRIPT CANNOT
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever written a text message or a line for class, read it back silently, thought it sounded fine, then said it out loud and immediately wanted to change a word?

A page rewards density: a reader can slow down, reread, and hold a complicated sentence in view as long as they need. A listener cannot. They get one pass, in real time, at the pace you set. A sentence built for the page, with three subordinate clauses and a qualification tucked inside a qualification, is often a sentence that only sounds finished when you read it silently. Say it aloud and you run out of breath, or the listener loses the subject before you reach the verb. This is not a flaw in your writing; it is a difference between two forms. Revising for the ear means testing every sentence the only way that actually tests it: aloud, at speed, once.

✏️ PART 2 · THREE REVISION MOVES
1. CUT THE WRITTEN-ONLY SENTENCE
A sentence that only works because a reader can see the punctuation (a long parenthetical, a semicolon holding two claims apart, a list of four items with commas) usually collapses when spoken. If you stumble on it twice in a row while recording, do not fix the wording; cut it, or split it into two short spoken sentences.
2. MOVE THE STRONGEST LINE EARLIER
A reader who loses interest can skim ahead to find your best line. A listener who loses interest simply stops listening. If your rough recording buries its strongest sentence three minutes in, move it, or an echo of its claim, into the first thirty seconds, so a listener has a reason to stay for the rest.
3. LET THE PRESERVED PASSAGE BREATHE
The moment you stop arguing and speak the preserved passage from memory is the episode's center of gravity. Do not rush into it or out of it. Leave a real pause before it starts and a real pause after it ends, on the page as much as in the recording, so the transition itself tells the listener something important is about to happen and just happened.

A revision pass wastes the least effort when it happens in a deliberate sequence. Put these four moves in the order that keeps you from polishing a line you are about to cut.

🤖 WORKING WITH AI

Claude can proofread the wording of your script for spelling and mechanics, but it has not heard you speak a single line of it. Whether a sentence trips the tongue or whether a pause lands right is something only your own recorded voice can tell you. Do the ear-test yourself before you finalize anything.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

The book people keep their texts alive by speaking them, not by filing them away unread. A script that only works silently is not finished; a script that survives being spoken aloud, once, without tripping, is close to done.
