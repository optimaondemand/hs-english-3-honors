📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Beatty keeps goading Montag even after the house is gone. Montag turns the flamethrower on him. This lesson reads that death soberly, as a fact the novel refuses to let anyone off for, including its own hero.
This is the hardest scene of the module, and the lesson treats it that way: no dwelling on the physical details of the death itself, and a direct focus on what the novel does with the fact of it afterward. Beatty, standing in the ruins of the house, keeps pushing Montag past what any person could absorb, and Montag kills him. The book gives you Montag's own later thought about why: "Beatty wanted to die." (Fahrenheit 451, Part Three). Today's reading tool is a caution against two easy readings, self-defense that erases the death entirely, or triumph that turns it into a victory, in favor of a third: a killing the novel insists Montag has to carry, whatever justification he finds for it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what leads Montag to kill Beatty and why the scene resists a single, comfortable reading.
2
I will be able to evaluate Montag's own explanation, "Beatty wanted to die," against the evidence in the scene.
3
I will be able to connect this death, in one paragraph, to Hamlet and the deaths his story causes.
📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
When a story lets its main character do something terrible for an understandable reason, should the reader still call it terrible? Or does understanding why change what it is?

Heroism that is not clean

Most stories about a character breaking free of a corrupt system let the reader cheer without complication. This one does not. Montag becomes, by the end of this scene, the character the plot has been building toward: a man who chooses books over obedience, in the most extreme way the plot can force. He is also, in the same scene, a killer, and the novel refuses to let either fact cancel the other. Read for both at once. Notice where the prose slows down to let you feel the weight of what has happened, rather than rushing past it to the next plot beat, and notice that Montag's own explanation afterward, that Beatty wanted this, may be true, may be a story Montag needs to tell himself, or may be both at once. The novel does not resolve which.

TWO READINGS THIS SCENE RESISTS, AND THE ONE IT ASKS FOR
Too easy: pure self-defense
Erases the death by treating it as fully justified and therefore not worth dwelling on.
Too easy: pure triumph
Turns a killing into a victory lap, ignoring what the text itself lingers on.
What the text asks for
Holding the reason and the weight together, and reading Montag's own explanation as evidence to weigh, not a verdict to accept.
📖 PART 2 · NOW READ, THEN COMPARE TO HAMLET

Continue in Part Three from where the house finishes burning through Beatty's goading in the ruins and Montag's act. Watch for: what specifically Beatty says that finally pushes Montag past his limit; how the narration handles the moment itself (where it lingers, where it moves quickly); and Montag's own later thought about why it happened.

📖 Read: Fahrenheit 451, Part Three, from the end of the house-burning through Montag's act against Beatty and his first steps away from the scene. In your own copy.

You have read one other story this year in which a single character causes a stage full of death, directly and indirectly, and the play never quite tells you whether to call him a hero for it: Hamlet. In one paragraph, connect the two. Both plots put their central figure in a position where thinking and hesitating has real costs, and both refuse the reader a clean verdict once the deaths actually happen. Neither story asks you to forgive its protagonist. Both ask you to keep watching him anyway.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING

Montag's own words for what happened, "Beatty wanted to die," are an interpretation, not a fact the novel confirms from outside him. Test that interpretation against what you actually read.

READING RESPONSE
Write out Montag's line "Beatty wanted to die." (Part Three). Then write a paragraph: do you find this explanation convincing, based on what Beatty actually says and does in the scene, or does it read more like something Montag needs to believe? Support your answer with one paraphrased detail from your own copy, cited (Part Three). Finish with your one-paragraph comparison to Hamlet: what does holding these two stories side by side tell you about how this year's texts have handled a protagonist who causes death and is never let off the hook for it?
