📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A girl asks a fireman whether he is happy. He laughs. Then he opens his bedroom door.
Bradbury builds character the way a photographer builds a portrait: by what he places next to it. Today's reading tool is characterization by contrast, reading two people as a pair so that each one shows what the other lacks. Clarisse McClellan, seventeen, walks at night, tastes the rain, and asks questions. Mildred Montag lies in the dark with radios in her ears and a screen in her head. Between them stands a man who has just been asked a question he cannot answer. You read the two women side by side, then read the night that follows, and decide what the novel means by happiness.

I will be able to read two characters as a deliberate pair and say what each one reveals about the other.
2

3
I will be able to explain what the society means by "happy" and what Clarisse means by it, with evidence from my own copy.

Characterization by contrast

A novelist has two ways to tell you who a person is. Direct characterization states it: she was curious, he was numb. Indirect characterization shows it and lets you conclude: what the person does with her hands, what fills her room, what she notices and fails to notice. Bradbury almost never states. He places, and he pairs. When two characters are introduced within a few pages and one is everything the other is not, the pairing is the argument. You read a foil (a character whose qualities set off another's by contrast) by asking, for each detail of one, where is the matching detail of the other, and what does the gap between them say. In this reading Clarisse is introduced outdoors, in motion, in weather; Mildred indoors, motionless, in the dark. The rooms are doing the characterizing.

READING A FOIL: FIVE PLACES TO LOOK FOR THE CONTRAST
Where we meet her
Outdoors or indoors? Moving or still? Light or dark?
What is in her ears and eyes
The rain and the moon, or a machine?
What she does with time
Walks and looks, or fills every hour with the walls?
How she talks to Montag
Questions he cannot answer, or answers to questions he did not ask?
What she remembers
A whole evening, or not even last night?
For each of the five, find one detail for Clarisse and one for Mildred in your copy. The gap between them is Bradbury's argument about happiness.
📖 PART 2 · NOW READ: MONTAG GOES INSIDE

Continue in Part One from Montag's front door: the dark bedroom, the sound of the jets, what he finds when he reaches for his wife, the two men with the machines who come in the night, and the next morning's breakfast, when Mildred remembers nothing. Read on through Montag's second meeting with Clarisse and her account of how she spends her days (the walks, the rain, the psychiatrist she is sent to), and stop when she asks whether he ever reads what he burns. Watch for: the objects that fill Mildred's room and what each one is for; the men's attitude toward the job they do in the bedroom; and what Clarisse does with her hands and her attention that no one else in the book has done so far.

📖 Read: Fahrenheit 451, Part One, from Montag's front door through his second conversation with Clarisse, ending at her question, "Do you ever read any of the books you burn?" (Fahrenheit 451, Part One). In your own copy.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING

Clarisse's question, "Are you happy?" (Fahrenheit 451, Part One), hangs over everything Montag sees when he goes inside. The society would answer yes without pausing. The bedroom answers differently.

📓
READING RESPONSE
Build the five-row foil grid from Part 1: for each of the five places to look, copy one short detail for Clarisse and one for Mildred from your own copy, each cited (Part One). Then write one paragraph answering: what does this society mean by "happy," and what does Clarisse mean by it? Point to the two details that best show the difference. End with one line on the module question: on the evidence so far, what has this society given up to feel happy?
