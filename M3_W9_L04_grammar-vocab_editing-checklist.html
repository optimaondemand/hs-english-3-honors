📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Editing is the last thing a reader never notices you did, and the first thing they notice if you skip it.
Revision covers shape and sentence. One last pass covers the small things: concision, consistency of headings and tense, agreement, quotation punctuation, and a final MLA check. This is a review week: no new Word Study. Instead, look back at every Word Study word from Module 3 and put it to one more use.

✅ PART 1 · THE FIVE-POINT EDITING CHECKLIST

Run this against the feature, in order, before saving it.

1. Concision
Read every sentence and ask whether a shorter version says the same thing. Cut "in order to" to "to," cut throat-clearing openers ("It is important to note that").
2. Consistency of headings and tense
Check your headings still share one grammatical shape (the text-features lesson). Check you are not sliding between present tense ("Jane feels") and past tense ("Jane felt") without reason; discussing the novel's events in present tense, consistently, is the standard convention.
3. Agreement
Subjects and verbs, pronouns and antecedents. A common slip in feature writing: "a governess" (singular) later becomes "they," which needs to be "she" or a rewritten, genuinely plural subject.
4. Quotation punctuation
Commas and periods inside closing quotation marks in American usage. A citation follows the closing quotation mark, outside it, before the sentence's final period: "…a nuisance" (ch. 17).
5. Final MLA check
Every in-text citation has a matching source-list entry, and every source-list entry is cited somewhere in the piece. No orphans on either side.

📕 PART 3 · WORDS OF THE MODULE (review)

No new Word Study this week. Instead, here is every word Module 3 has collected, week by week. Pick at least three and use them, correctly, somewhere in a revision.

WeekWord(s)Copia challenge
1opprobrium, sanguine (ch. 2)Use opprobrium for a public blame that falls on the wrong person, and sanguine for a confidence that has not yet been tested.
2privation (ch. 7), benefactress (ch. 8)Use privation to name a lack, not just a hardship, and benefactress to show a kindness that still carries a power imbalance.
3physiognomy (ch. 13), tête-à-tête (ch. 14)Use physiognomy for a face read as a sign of character, and the borrowed phrase tête-à-tête for a conversation whose privacy is the point.
4isolation (ch. 17), incredulity (ch. 18)Use isolation for a solitude imposed by circumstance, not chosen, and incredulity for a disbelief strong enough to show on a face.
5vehemence (ch. 23), degradation (ch. 24 & 27)Use vehemence for a force of feeling that shows in how something is said, not just what, and degradation for a lowering that is felt, not only stated.
6mendicant (ch. 28), philanthropy (ch. 32)Use mendicant precisely, for someone reduced to begging, and philanthropy for a generosity aimed at humanity broadly, not one person.
7conflagration (ch. 36), indefatigable (ch. 38)Use conflagration for a fire large enough to be an event, not a household accident, and indefatigable for an effort that does not visibly tire.
8appanage, superannuated (Fraser's Magazine, 1844)Use appanage for something treated as automatically belonging to a role, and superannuated for someone set aside by age with no provision made.
