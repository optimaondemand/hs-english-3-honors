📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A heading is a promise. If the section under it does not keep that promise, the reader stops trusting every heading after it.
You have the anatomy of a feature and you know how to write for a stranger. Today is the conventions that make those parts actually work on the page: how a heading has to match its section, why headings in one feature share the same grammar, how to punctuate a pull-quote and a caption, how to write numbers and dates so they read cleanly, and the source-list format for both a JStor source and the periodical text. Then Word Study: two words from the 1844 periodical, appanage and superannuated.

✏️ PART 1 · HEADINGS AS PROMISES, AND PARALLEL HEADING GRAMMAR

A heading is a promise about what comes next; break it and the reader stops trusting the rest of your headings, too. A set of headings also needs to share one grammatical shape, called parallel structure, so a skimming reader's eye can move down the list of headings and feel one consistent rhythm rather than four different sentence types.

NOT PARALLEL · FOUR DIFFERENT SHAPES
What Did a Governess Earn?
The Isolation of the Position
How Families Treated Their Children's Teacher
Escape
A question, a noun phrase, a full sentence, and a single word. The reader has to reset expectations at every heading.
PARALLEL · ONE SHAPE, HELD
What a Governess Earned
Why the Position Was Isolating
How Families Treated Their Children's Teacher
Why So Few Ways Out Existed
All four are indirect questions built the same way ("what/why/how" plus a clause). The reader learns the pattern once and rides it through the whole feature.
📝 PART 2 · CAPTIONS, PULL-QUOTES, NUMBERS, AND DATES

Captions identify an image and its source in one line: subject, then artist or origin, then date, then the fact that it is public domain if it is. Example: Fraser's Magazine, title page, November 1844. Public domain, Internet Archive. A caption is not a sentence of analysis; save that for your body text.

Pull-quotes are set apart from the body, so they do not need their own quotation marks if the design already makes clear they are lifted text, but a source citation still travels with them. Pull a genuinely striking sentence, not the flattest one that happens to fit the space.

Numbers and dates in feature prose follow a simple rule: spell out numbers under 100 in running text ("thirty-five pounds a year"), use numerals for exact statistics and all years ("1844," "351.," which the essay itself renders as an old-style currency figure you would modernize to "£35"), and write full dates without ordinals ("14 November 1844," not "14th of November, 1844").

🔖 PART 3 · THE SOURCE LIST'S FORMAT

A feature's source list is MLA, alphabetized, hanging-indented, with an entry for every source you cite: the novel, your JStor source, and, if you use it, the periodical text.

SOURCE LIST · MODEL ENTRIES
Brontë, Charlotte. Jane Eyre: An Autobiography. 1847. Project Gutenberg, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1260.
"Hints on the Modern Governess System." Fraser's Magazine, vol. 30, no. 179, Nov. 1844, pp. 571–83. Internet Archive, archive.org/details/sim_frasers-magazine_1844-11_30_179.
Author Last Name, First Name. "Title of the Article." Name of the Scholarly Journal, vol. number, no. number, year, pages. JSTOR, stable address from the article page.

A source with no named author, like the anonymous 1844 essay, alphabetizes by the first word of its title, skipping "A" or "The."

📕 PART 4 · WORD STUDY (from the periodical text)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
appanage
noun		 On why a governess had become common in wealthy households: "In process of time, therefore, a governess became a necessary appanage in every family." ("Hints on the Modern Governess System," Fraser's Magazine, Nov. 1844, p. 572) Something that regularly and naturally belongs to a person or household, a fixture that comes with a certain rank or status, as if attached to it by right. The word carries a hint of the essay's argument: a governess is treated as a household fixture, an accessory of status, rather than as a person with her own claims. From French apanage, ultimately from Latin ad- ("to") and panis ("bread"): originally the land or income a noble family granted a younger son to provide his "bread," his living, apart from the main inheritance.
superannuated
adjective		 On what became of a governess too old to work: "What is to become of those women who, after toiling out the best of their days, survive to old age?… the superannuated governess has not even these [the poor-laws and unions available to the lower classes]." ("Hints on the Modern Governess System," Fraser's Magazine, Nov. 1844, p. 575) Retired or set aside because of old age, and no longer useful in a role, often with no provision made for what comes after. The essay uses it to name a real gap: there was no pension system built for governesses the way there was, however roughly, for other classes of worker. From Latin super annum, "beyond a year," by way of the university term superannuatus, a student who had stayed enrolled beyond the normal span of years without finishing; the sense narrowed over time to mean simply "too old for the position."
📓
WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For each word, write one new sentence of your own on a subject unrelated to the novel or the essay, keeping the meaning exact. For appanage, show something treated as an automatic accessory of a role, not just call it "typical." For superannuated, show a person or thing set aside for age without provision made for what follows; do not water it down to just "old." Then a copia move: rewrite the essay's superannuated governess sentence with a plainer phrase in its place, and note in one line what the plainer version loses.
💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Every convention today exists for the same reader: the stranger who owes the writer nothing. A parallel heading, a properly punctuated caption, a source list someone could actually use, none of it is decoration. It is what lets a reader trust you enough to keep going.
