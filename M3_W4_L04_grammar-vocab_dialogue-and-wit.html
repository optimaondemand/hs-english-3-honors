📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
“Why don't you tremble?” “I'm not cold.” Four words answer four words, and the exchange never slows down.
This week's chapters are full of the sharpest talk in the novel so far. Today you learn the mechanics that make dialogue readable on the page, quotation marks, the comma before a tag, and how to punctuate a line that gets cut off, all using Jane and Rochester's own exchanges. Then Word Study: two words from chapters 16 through 21, sardonic and presentiment.

✏️ PART 1 · THE MECHANICS OF A GOOD LINE OF DIALOGUE

Three rules carry almost all of Brontë's dialogue. The tag rule: when a speech tag (she said, he asked) follows a line of dialogue, close the quotation with a comma, not a period, and keep the tag lowercase unless it starts with a proper noun. The new-speaker rule: every time the speaker changes, start a new paragraph, even if the new line is only one word long; that paragraph break is what lets a reader's eye follow a fast exchange without a single "she said" to guide them. The interruption rule: when a speaker is cut off, either by another character breaking in or by the narration itself, Brontë ends the unfinished line with a dash and picks up the sentence outside the quotation marks. Your own prose in this course keeps to periods, colons, and semicolons rather than dashes; when you punctuate interrupted dialogue in the widget and your response task below, use the dash exactly as it appears in Brontë's own sentences, since that convention belongs to the quoted dialogue itself, not to your own analytical writing.

THREE RULES, IN BRONTË'S OWN LINES
THE TAG RULE · comma inside the closing quotation mark, lowercase tag
“Well, and you want your fortune told?” she said, in a voice as decided as her glance, as harsh as her features. (Jane Eyre, ch. 19)
The comma sits inside the quotation marks; the tag that follows stays lowercase because it is finishing one sentence, not starting a new one.
THE NEW-SPEAKER RULE · a fresh paragraph for every change of speaker
“Am I wanted?” I asked.
“Are you up?” asked the voice I expected to hear…
“Yes, sir.”
“And dressed?”
“Yes.”
“Come out, then, quietly.” (Jane Eyre, ch. 20)
Six lines, six paragraph breaks, and only two of them even need a tag. The white space does the work of telling you who is speaking.
THE INTERRUPTION RULE · a dash inside the quotation marks, then narration outside them
“Let her be taken care of; let her be treated as tenderly as may be: let her—” he stopped and burst into tears. (Jane Eyre, ch. 20)
The dash stops the sentence mid-thought, inside the quotation marks; what comes after it is the narrator telling you what happened, not more of the speech.
One more test for the short reply that lands: it works because the line before it was longer. “I'm not cold.” only registers as a blow because the question that provoked it took the trouble to ask.

READING RESPONSE
Write a six-line exchange of dialogue between two characters of your own invention, arguing about something small (whose turn it is to do a chore, who was right about a bet). Apply all three rules: at least one line uses a speech tag with the comma placed correctly, every change of speaker gets its own paragraph, and one line is interrupted, either by the other speaker or by a narration beat, using a dash exactly as Brontë does inside the quotation marks. 📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapters 16–21)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
sardonic
adjective		 Told that Mrs. Poole's charge might have to be dealt with, Mr. Rochester's mood turns bitter: "He laughed sardonically, hastily took my hand, and as hastily threw it from him." (Jane Eyre, ch. 20) Mocking and bitterly scornful, especially in a laugh or a smile that has no real amusement in it. A sardonic laugh is not shared with anyone; it is aimed, often at the speaker's own situation as much as at anyone else. Notice how quickly Brontë follows the laugh with a physical gesture, taking a hand and throwing it away, that acts out the same bitterness. Disputed. Long explained as coming from a Sardinian plant said to convulse the face into a ghastly grin when eaten; more likely the word grew from the Greek sardanios, a bitter or scornful expression, later linked to Sardinia by popular confusion. Dictionaries still flag the exact history as unsettled.
presentiment
noun		 Before the chapter has even named what has happened, the narrator opens with a claim about foreboding: "Presentiments are strange things! and so are sympathies; and so are signs." (Jane Eyre, ch. 21) A vague sense that something, usually something bad, is about to happen, felt before any actual evidence arrives. A presentiment is not a prediction built on facts; it is a feeling that runs ahead of the facts, which is exactly why the narrator spends a paragraph justifying trusting hers before she tells you what it warned her about. From French pressentiment, built on Latin prae- ("before") and sentire ("to feel, to sense"). The word means, quite literally, a feeling that arrives before its cause is known.

WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For each word, write one new sentence of your own on a subject unrelated to the novel, keeping the meaning exact. For sardonic, make sure your sentence shows the bitterness, not just ordinary sarcasm; a sardonic remark or expression should feel like it costs the speaker something. For presentiment, write a sentence where the feeling comes first and the reason for it is either withheld or arrives later, the way the chapter withholds its own reason for a paragraph. Then a copia move: rewrite the sardonic-laugh sentence above using a plainer synonym in place of sardonically, and note in one line what the plainer word loses.
