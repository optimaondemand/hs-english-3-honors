📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A sentence that reads perfectly on a page can still lose a listener the moment it is spoken aloud. The eye can hold a long sentence in view. The ear cannot go back and check.
This lesson applies grammar directly to a spoken script, the last editing pass before recording. It covers why sentence length behaves differently for a listener than a reader, how to run the read-aloud test on one's own writing, which words exist only on the page and need to be cut or replaced for a spoken script, and the discipline of the one-idea sentence. Since this is the module's last new grammar and vocabulary lesson, all twelve Word Study words from across the module are reviewed in one table.

🎤 PART 1 · THE READ-ALOUD TEST
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever read something silently and understood it fine, then stumbled over the exact same sentence the moment you tried to say it out loud? What usually trips you up?

The read-aloud test is simple and non-negotiable for a script: read every sentence out loud, exactly as written, before you record it. If you run out of breath before the sentence ends, it is too long for a listener's ear, whatever it looks like on the page. If you stumble on the same word twice, that word is probably wrong for speech, not just hard for you personally. A script that has never been read aloud before recording has not actually been edited yet.

✏️ PART 2 · SENTENCE LENGTH, WRITTEN-ONLY WORDS, AND THE ONE-IDEA SENTENCE

A reader can pause anywhere, reread a clause, or glance back at a word three lines up. A listener gets one pass, in real time, at the speed you choose. That single difference explains every rule below.

WRITTEN FOR THE PAGE, THEN EDITED FOR THE EAR
Both versions below were written for this lesson; they are not from any student's episode.
WRITTEN FOR THE PAGE (one 41-word sentence, three ideas)
The subsequent implication of this particular incident, which occurred in a manner that many contemporaneous observers found genuinely astonishing, was that the previously established consensus regarding the matter would henceforth require substantial and immediate reconsideration.
EDITED FOR THE EAR (three short sentences, one idea each)
Something surprising happened. It shook what everyone thought they knew. The old answer no longer held.
Notice what changed: three ideas, once chained into one long sentence, became three short sentences, one idea each. Written-only words (subsequent, contemporaneous, henceforth) were replaced with words a speaker actually says out loud. Nothing true was lost; the listener simply gets to absorb one idea before the next one arrives.

Three edits, run on every sentence of a draft: Count the ideas. If a sentence holds more than one, split it. Read it for breath. If you cannot say it in one comfortable breath, shorten it. Listen for stiffness. If a word would look at home in a formal essay but strange in conversation, replace it with the word you would actually say to a friend.

📕 PART 3 · WORD STUDY REVIEW: THE MODULE'S TWELVE WORDS

This is a review, not new material: every word below was already taught in an earlier week. For each, a one-line copia challenge, an invitation to say the same idea a new way, exactly the skill your script revision needs this week.

WordWeekQuick reminderCopia challenge (say it again, a new way)
mausoleum1A grand tomb; used as a chilling comparison for a roomDescribe an ordinary room as if it were built to hold the dead, without using the word mausoleum itself.
olfactory1Of the sense of smell; a clinical, instrument-like wordDescribe a machine's sense of smell in clinical, instrument-like language, then again in warm, animal language. Notice the difference.
noncombustible2Not able to be set on fire or burnedName three ordinary objects that are noncombustible and explain, in one sentence, why that quality would matter to someone afraid of fire.
proboscis2A long, flexible feeding or sensing organ, animal or insectDescribe an insect's or animal's feeding organ using proboscis, then describe the same feature the way a young child would, with no technical word at all.
dictum3A formal, authoritative statement of principleState a short rule you actually live by as a formal dictum, then restate the same rule as casual advice to a friend.
verbatim3In exactly the same words; word for wordCopy one sentence from any source verbatim, then paraphrase the same sentence in your own words, and explain in one line what changed and what stayed the same.
gadfly4A persistent critic who provokes others to think or actDescribe a real person, historical or contemporary, who plays the social role of a gadfly, and explain in one sentence why that role tends to be unwelcome.
reproach4An expression of disapproval or blameWrite one sentence in which a person's expression, not their words, delivers a reproach.
incinerate5To burn completely to ashUse incinerate in a sentence about something other than a book or a house, so the word's precise, complete-destruction sense stays sharp.
spectacle5A public display meant to be watched, often striking or dramaticDescribe one event as a spectacle meant to be watched, then describe the same event as it might feel to someone forced to be inside it rather than watching.
vanity6Emptiness or lack of lasting substance (King James sense); also excessive self-regard (modern sense)Use vanity once in its older, King James sense (emptiness) and once in its common modern sense (excessive self-regard), and note the shift in one line.
travail6Hard, wearing labor; toil that costs body and spiritUse travail once in a sentence about work you chose on purpose, so the reader feels the cost and the choice at once.

READING RESPONSE
Take one paragraph of your own broadcast script and run the three edits from Part 2 on it: split any multi-idea sentence, cut or replace any written-only word, and check every sentence with the breath test. Paste the before and after side by side. Then complete at least four of the twelve copia challenges above, your choice of which four, and record your sentences in your Word Study table.
