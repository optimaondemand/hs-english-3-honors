📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A script that reads beautifully on the page can still fall flat in someone's ears. A listener cannot reread a sentence they lost; you have to earn their attention in real time.
The novel is finished, and the work shifts from reading new pages to producing: turning a written script into something a listener can actually follow by ear alone. This lesson covers four production skills: voice, pace, pitch, and pause; how to mark a script so the performance can be remembered; how to transition between segments without losing the listener; and what can and cannot be used for music or sound.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to mark a script for breath, stress, and pace before recording it.
2
I will be able to build a transition between two segments that a listener can follow without visual cues.
3
I will be able to name what sound and music I am and am not allowed to use, and why.
🎤 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a podcast, audiobook, or narrated video you actually enjoyed listening to. What did the voice do that kept you listening, beyond just reading the words correctly?

The four levers: voice, pace, pitch, pause

A spoken script has four controls a written one does not. Voice is the character or tone you adopt (a host explaining, a host arguing, a host reciting); it can change segment to segment on purpose. Pace is how quickly words arrive; a fast pace builds urgency, a slow one gives weight. Pitch is the rise and fall of your voice within a sentence; a flat pitch reads as bored or robotic no matter how good the words are. Pause is silence used on purpose, before a turn, after a hard fact, or in place of a comma the ear cannot see. None of these exist on the page until you mark them there yourself, which is exactly what Part 2 teaches you to do.

✍️ PART 2 · ANNOTATING A SCRIPT FOR PERFORMANCE

Radio and podcast scripts use a small, informal set of marks to remind a performer, mid-recording, what they decided in rehearsal. You do not need special software; a pencil, or the comment feature of a word processor, is enough. Study the marked-up excerpt below, written for this lesson to model the technique, not to be read as part of any student's actual episode.

A MARKED SCRIPT EXCERPT (written for this lesson, not a student example)
HOST, cold open segment. Slashes mark a breath; bold marks the stressed word; bracketed notes mark pace or a pause.
In nineteen fifty-five, / a man in Montgomery, Alabama / refused to give up his seat. // [slow down] He was not the first person / to make that choice. / He was the first one / the city could not afford / to ignore. [pause, two full seconds] // Tonight, / we are keeping his refusal alive. [pick pace back up]
Key: a single slash / marks a light breath, a comma-length pause. A double slash // marks a fuller stop, longer than a period would suggest on the page. Bold marks the one word in a sentence that should carry the most stress; a sentence usually has only one, and choosing it is a real interpretive decision. Bracketed notes in the margin remind you of a pace or pause choice you made in rehearsal, so you do not have to re-decide it live.

Transitions between segments. A listener cannot see a scene change the way a viewer can. Mark every transition explicitly in your script: a line that signals what is ending and what is beginning ("That is the cold open. Now, the passage I promised you."), a short pause, and, if you are using sound, a brief musical or tonal shift rather than an abrupt cut. Weak transitions are the single most common reason a produced episode feels confusing to a first-time listener; strong ones are often only one sentence long.

🎵 A PLAIN NOTE ON MUSIC AND SOUND
Any music or sound effect in your episode must be royalty-free or self-made, and credited in your show notes. Licensed commercial music, even a short clip, cannot be used; the license does not cover a student project distributed outside the copyright holder's terms. Plenty of royalty-free libraries exist for exactly this purpose, and a self-made sound (a recorded door, a hummed tone, a tapped rhythm) is always acceptable and always yours to credit as your own. Silence, used well, is also a legitimate production choice and needs no license at all.
