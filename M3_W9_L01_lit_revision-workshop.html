📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A draft has everything it needs and nothing it doesn't yet. That second part is the work of revision.
A full draft, and likely notes from a teacher, make the starting point. Revision is not proofreading; it is re-seeing, in order: cut what does not serve a stranger, reorder what is out of sequence, then sharpen the sentences that carry the weight. You already own the tools for the sharpening pass: the periodic sentence, the cumulative sentence, and the short blow from the periodic-sentence lesson. They serve here as revision moves, not just as things to recognize.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to revise a feature in the order that avoids wasted work: cut, reorder, sharpen.
2
I will be able to use a periodic sentence, a cumulative sentence, and a short blow as deliberate revision choices in my lead.
3
I will be able to apply a revision checklist to my own draft before finalizing it.
🔄 PART 1 · CUT, REORDER, SHARPEN, IN THAT ORDER
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
If you polish a sentence's wording first and then decide to cut the whole paragraph it lives in, what happened to the time you spent polishing?

Revise in three passes, in this order, so you never polish a sentence you later cut.

REVISE IN THREE PASSES
Pass 1 · Cut
Find every sentence that does not pass the stranger test or does not serve the one idea of its section. Cut it. A shorter feature that a stranger finishes beats a longer one they abandon.
Pass 2 · Reorder
Check your section order against your headings. Does the reader need one idea explained before another makes sense? Move sections, and check your pull-quote is still pulled from the section that survives around it.
Pass 3 · Sharpen
Only now work sentence by sentence, using the three shapes below on your lead and your strongest section.
✏️ PART 2 · THREE SENTENCE SHAPES AS REVISION MOVES

Back in Week 1 you learned to recognize the periodic sentence, the cumulative sentence, and the short blow in Brontë's prose. In your lead, they become choices you make on purpose.

PERIODIC, TO BUILD SUSPENSE BEFORE A FACT
Hold a striking detail to the end of a sentence, so the reader arrives at it rather than being told it up front.
CUMULATIVE, TO FILL IN A PICTURE
State your claim first, then add qualifying detail after detail, so a skimming reader gets the point even if they stop partway through.
THE SHORT BLOW, TO CLOSE A LEAD
End your lead paragraph with a short, plain sentence. After a periodic or cumulative build, it lands, and it is often the best place for your pull-quote-worthy line.

Example revision: a flat lead like "Governesses in Victorian novels had a hard life and this essay will explain why" can become, using the same three tools: "She earns her keep, obeys the household's every rule, and still eats apart from the family she serves. In 1847, that contradiction had a name: governess." A cumulative build, then a short blow.

✅ PART 3 · THE REVISION CHECKLIST

Run this checklist against a full draft before finalizing it. Put the steps in the order they belong; the order is the point.

🔘 PUT THE REVISION STEPS IN ORDER
Click the four moves in the order a disciplined revision follows.
📓
READING RESPONSE
Write down what you cut, what you reordered, and why. List at least two sentences or passages you removed and the reason each failed the stranger test or its section's one-idea rule, then note any section you moved and what made the new order clearer. Finish by rewriting your lead's closing sentence as a short blow, if it is not one already.
🤖 WORKING WITH AI

Claude can proofread spelling and mechanics honestly, a fair use in your final pass. It cannot verify your sources: it can invent a plausible citation that does not exist, so every source list entry must come from a source you opened yourself. And do not let it rewrite your lead; a generic AI sentence in the middle of your own voice is easy for a reader to spot.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Brontë revised too; the woman who wrote "Currer Bell" on a title page checked every sentence before she let a stranger read it. Revision is not a punishment for a rough draft; it is the last, necessary act of taking your reader seriously.
