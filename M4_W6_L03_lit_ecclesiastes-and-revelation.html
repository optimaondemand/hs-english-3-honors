📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Of all the texts a man might carry in his memory to the end of the world, Montag settles on two: a chapter about time and mortality, and a vision of a river lined with a tree whose leaves heal.
As the group walks toward the ruined city, Montag finds himself holding two passages from the Bible in his memory: Ecclesiastes 3 and the opening verses of Revelation 22. Both are public domain and both are printed in full below, so that you can read exactly what Montag is carrying rather than a description of it. Today's lesson asks why a society trying to speak to a broken world would reach for these two passages in particular, and what each one offers a people who have just watched their own memory burn.

📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
If you could only carry two short passages of text in memory to comfort and guide a group of survivors after a disaster, would you look for something that explains suffering, or something that promises healing? Why?

Reading tool: a text with two audiences

Both passages below were written for readers of their own time, and both now arrive at Montag secondhand, remembered rather than read from a page. The reading tool for today is to ask of each passage two questions at once: what did it originally offer its own audience, and what does it offer a fictional group of survivors who have no other book left to open. Holding both questions together is exactly the kind of double reading Bradbury is asking of you: these are real, historical texts, doing new work inside a novel that did not write a single word of them.

📖 READ IT YOURSELF: ECCLESIASTES 3

What to watch for: the list of paired opposites in the opening verses, and the question the chapter asks right after that list, about what any of that labor is actually for.

📖 Read: Ecclesiastes 3 (King James Version, public domain, Project Gutenberg #10)

3:1 To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: 3:2 A time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted; 3:3 A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up; 3:4 A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance; 3:5 A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing; 3:6 A time to get, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to cast away; 3:7 A time to rend, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak; 3:8 A time to love, and a time to hate; a time of war, and a time of peace.

3:9 What profit hath he that worketh in that wherein he laboureth? 3:10 I have seen the travail, which God hath given to the sons of men to be exercised in it.

3:11 He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end.

3:12 I know that there is no good in them, but for a man to rejoice, and to do good in his life.

3:13 And also that every man should eat and drink, and enjoy the good of all his labour, it is the gift of God.

3:14 I know that, whatsoever God doeth, it shall be for ever: nothing can be put to it, nor any thing taken from it: and God doeth it, that men should fear before him.

3:15 That which hath been is now; and that which is to be hath already been; and God requireth that which is past.

3:16 And moreover I saw under the sun the place of judgment, that wickedness was there; and the place of righteousness, that iniquity was there.

3:17 I said in mine heart, God shall judge the righteous and the wicked: for there is a time there for every purpose and for every work.

3:18 I said in mine heart concerning the estate of the sons of men, that God might manifest them, and that they might see that they themselves are beasts.

3:19 For that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts; even one thing befalleth them: as the one dieth, so dieth the other; yea, they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity.

3:20 All go unto one place; all are of the dust, and all turn to dust again.

3:21 Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth? 3:22 Wherefore I perceive that there is nothing better, than that a man should rejoice in his own works; for that is his portion: for who shall bring him to see what shall be after him?

📖 READ IT YOURSELF: REVELATION 22:1–7

What to watch for: what the tree's leaves are for, and what condition the passage says will no longer exist by the river.

📖 Read: Revelation 22:1–7 (King James Version, public domain, Project Gutenberg #10)

22:1 And he shewed me a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb.

22:2 In the midst of the street of it, and on either side of the river, was there the tree of life, which bare twelve manner of fruits, and yielded her fruit every month: and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations.

22:3 And there shall be no more curse: but the throne of God and of the Lamb shall be in it; and his servants shall serve him: 22:4 And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads.

22:5 And there shall be no night there; and they need no candle, neither light of the sun; for the Lord God giveth them light: and they shall reign for ever and ever.

22:6 And he said unto me, These sayings are faithful and true: and the Lord God of the holy prophets sent his angel to shew unto his servants the things which must shortly be done.

22:7 Behold, I come quickly: blessed is he that keepeth the sayings of the prophecy of this book.

📖 PART 2 · WHY THESE TWO PASSAGES

Neither passage was written for this novel's situation, and that is part of the point: the book people cannot write anything new, so they reach for words already made and let those words do new work. Ecclesiastes 3 offers a hard, steadying kind of comfort. It does not promise that suffering will stop; it insists instead that time itself has a shape, that building follows breaking the way planting follows uprooting, and that a person can find real good in ordinary labor and enjoyment even inside that pattern. It is the passage of a people who must first accept that loss has already happened and could not be undone by wishing.

Revelation 22:1–7 offers something Ecclesiastes withholds: a picture, not just an acceptance. A river, a tree that never stops bearing fruit, leaves whose purpose is explicitly to heal, and a promise that a certain curse will end. Read plainly, next to Ecclesiastes, the pairing moves from "this is the shape suffering and labor take" to "and here, still, is what healing could look like." A society with no future to imagine cannot rebuild anything; these two passages, held together, give the book people both the discipline to accept what has happened and an image, however old and borrowed, of what repair might actually look like.

TWO PASSAGES, TWO KINDS OF HELP FOR A SOCIETY THAT HAS LOST ITS MEMORY
ECCLESIASTES 3 offers…
Acceptance. A shape for time that includes breaking as well as building, so that loss is not treated as senseless, only as one half of a pattern that also includes repair.
REVELATION 22:1–7 offers…
A picture. A specific image of healing (the tree, the leaves, the river) for a people who need something concrete to walk toward, not only a pattern to accept.
