📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A camp by a set of abandoned railroad tracks. A circle of men around a small fire. Each one, when he introduces himself, gives you the name of a book instead of a name.
Montag crosses the river and climbs the bank into the company of Granger and a handful of men who used to be professors, writers, and readers. They have no books with them, and they do not need any: each man has committed one whole text to memory, word for word, and waits for a moment when the world will want it back. Today's lesson asks what it means to preserve a text by becoming it, and why this novel, of all the ones you have read this year, ends by handing that idea to a circle of men in the woods rather than to a library.

📚 PART 1 · A WARM-UP BEFORE WE BEGIN
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN
If every physical copy of one text you love vanished tonight, and the only way to save it was to memorize it word for word, would you attempt it? What would make that worth doing, or not worth doing?

Reading tool: a person read as a book

All year you have practiced reading a text closely: a soliloquy, a paper of Publius, a paragraph of Emerson, a chapter of Brontë. This lesson asks you to do something stranger: read a person the way you would read a book. When Granger introduces the men around the fire, he does not give their former jobs first. He gives the title each one carries. The reading tool is to ask, for each man you meet: what text does he hold, why that one, and what does the choice reveal about him. A person who has become a book is still a person, with a history and a grief of his own; the novel asks you to hold both facts at once.

📖 PART 2 · GRANGER'S CIRCLE

Granger leads a group of former academics, writers, and ordinary readers who live outside the city, moving on foot along old railroad lines. None of them carries a physical book; the firemen would only burn it. Instead, each man has memorized one complete text and, in effect, become it for as long as he is needed. Granger explains the method to Montag as a network rather than a library: a person who wants to preserve a hundred books needs a hundred people, each one holding a single text lightly enough to still be a person and firmly enough that the text survives him.

HOW THE BOOK PEOPLE PRESERVE A TEXT
STEP 1
Choose
A person picks, or is given, one text worth the years it will take to hold it whole.
→
STEP 2
Memorize
Not a summary. The words themselves, in their own order, kept exact.
→
STEP 3
Carry
Live an ordinary life, on foot, unarmed, waiting, the text carried and never spoken unless it is needed.
→
STEP 4
Pass it on
When paper and printing return, dictate the text to a new page, and start memorizing the next one.

Granger explains why the group does not simply try to save what already exists. His grandfather taught him a principle he now passes to the circle: “Everyone must leave something behind when he dies, my grandfather said.” (Fahrenheit 451, Part Three). A book is one way to leave something behind. A memorized book, carried by a living person until it can be written down again, is another, stranger way, and it is the only way left to this group.

Late in this section, Granger quotes his grandfather again, a fragment he does not finish, offered almost like a piece of advice passed hand to hand: “Stuff your eyes with wonder,” (Fahrenheit 451, Part Three). The line is given exactly as a fragment in the novel. Do not extend it or guess at what follows; read it as it stands and ask what a fragment, of all things, is doing at the center of a chapter about memorizing whole books.

Now read

Open your copy to where Montag reaches the far bank of the river in Part Three, “Burning Bright.” Read through his meeting with Granger's circle around the fire, the introductions and what each man carries, and Granger's account of the method and his grandfather. Watch for: what Montag himself is asked to become, now that he no longer has Faber's earpiece or a physical book of his own; which man's chosen text surprises you most, and why; and every place the passage above (the grandfather's two sayings) appears in your own copy, so you can quote the exact sentences.

📖 Read: Fahrenheit 451, Part Three, from Montag's crossing of the river to the end of the meeting at the fire, in your own copy.

✍️ PART 4 · DO SOMETHING WITH THE READING

The book people speak what they carry when someone finally needs to hear it, the way a soliloquy, an abolitionist's oratory, or a Victorian poem might be spoken from memory.

📓
READING RESPONSE
Write out both of Granger's grandfather's lines, cited (Part Three): the full sentence about leaving something behind, and the fragment about wonder, exactly as it stands, with no words added to finish it. Then write one paragraph: what does the book people's method say about what a text needs from a reader, that a library on a shelf does not need? Tie the answer to a passage carried by heart this year: what has been carried since the first one, and what has changed for having carried it?
