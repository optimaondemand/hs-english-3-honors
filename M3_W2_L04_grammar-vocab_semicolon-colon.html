📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"But this is not to be a regular autobiography: I am only bound to invoke Memory where I know her responses will possess some degree of interest." One sentence, two different marks, two different jobs.
Brontë builds her long sentences out of clauses chained together, and two marks do almost all of that chaining: the semicolon, which joins two complete statements that belong together, and the colon, which opens a statement onto its own explanation. Today you learn to tell them apart in her own prose from this week's chapters, then Word Study: two words from chapters 5 through 10, veneration and mortify.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a semicolon joining two complete statements from a colon opening a statement onto its explanation, in Brontë's own sentences.
2
I will be able to write one sentence of my own using a semicolon correctly and one using a colon correctly.
3
I will be able to use veneration and mortify precisely in sentences of my own.
✏️ PART 1 · TWO MARKS, TWO JOBS

A semicolon joins two complete statements, each one able to stand alone as its own sentence, when the two are close enough in meaning to belong in the same breath. A colon does something different: it opens a complete statement onto whatever explains, illustrates, or completes it, whether that is one word, a list, or another full statement. The test for a semicolon is simple: could you drop a period in its place and still have two sentences? If yes, the semicolon is doing its job. The test for a colon is just as simple: does what follows the colon answer the question "what do you mean by that?" If yes, the colon is doing its job. Brontë often uses both in the same long sentence, moving from a colon's explanation into a semicolon's chain of parallel clauses.

ONE SENTENCE, BOTH MARKS AT WORK (CHAPTER 10)
But this is not to be a regular autobiography: I am only bound to invoke Memory where I know her responses will possess some degree of interest; therefore I now pass a space of eight years almost in silence: a few lines only are necessary to keep up the links of connection. (Jane Eyre, ch. 10)
Colon
Opens the claim ("not a regular autobiography") onto its explanation: what she means, and later, her conclusion.
Semicolon
Joins two complete statements, cause and effect, that belong in the same breath: her rule, then what she does because of it.
Read it aloud: the colons make you lean in for an answer; the semicolon lets you keep moving without a full stop.

📕 PART 2 · WORD STUDY (from chapters 5 and 7)
WordIn the textWhat it meansWord origin
veneration
noun		 Jane watching Miss Temple cross the schoolroom for the first time: "I suppose I have a considerable organ of veneration, for I retain yet the sense of admiring awe with which my eyes traced her steps." (Jane Eyre, ch. 5) Deep respect and reverence, the feeling due to someone whose character or position earns awe rather than mere liking. Jane uses it about herself almost clinically, as if respect were a faculty she happens to possess in quantity, and then spends the rest of the sentence proving it: tracing Miss Temple's steps with "admiring awe." Notice the word appears again in chapter 9, when Helen construes a page of Virgil and Jane's "organ of veneration" expands at every line: the word marks the two people Jane most looks up to at Lowood. From Latin venerari, "to revere, to worship," related to venus, veneris ("charm, beauty, desirability"). The root sense is a pull toward something that inspires awe, later narrowed to the specific feeling of reverent respect.
mortify
verb		 Mr. Brocklehurst explaining his purpose to Miss Temple: "I have a Master to serve whose kingdom is not of this world: my mission is to mortify in these girls the lusts of the flesh; to teach them to clothe themselves with shame-facedness and sobriety, not with braided hair and costly apparel." (Jane Eyre, ch. 7) To deliberately deny or subdue bodily desire, treating comfort and vanity as things to be starved out. Brocklehurst uses the word in its older religious sense, not its more common modern one ("to embarrass"): he means an active, disciplined killing-off of appetite, dressed up as spiritual discipline. The word is doing real work in his sentence, since his own family's silk and curls, minutes later, show exactly whose flesh he does not mean to mortify. From Latin mortificare, "to put to death," from mors, mortis ("death") plus facere ("to make, to do"). Literally, to make dead: the word still carries that root sense of a small, deliberate killing, even in its modern, weaker use for embarrassment.
📓
WORD STUDY · TRY IT
For veneration, write a sentence about a real person or achievement you genuinely respect, letting the sentence show the awe rather than just naming it. For mortify, write a sentence using the word's older, stronger sense (a deliberate denial of comfort or desire), not the weaker modern sense of embarrassment; make the distinction clear in how you use it. Then a copia move: rewrite Brocklehurst's mortify sentence replacing the word with a plainer synonym, and note in one line what the plainer word loses. Notice how both words still carry their Latin roots close to the surface: venus inside veneration, mors inside mortify.
💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Writing for a stranger who cannot see the writer's face or hear the writer's tone calls for plain tools. A colon that opens a claim onto its proof, and a semicolon that joins two related facts without a full stop, keep an explainer moving without losing the reader. Practice them as habits.
